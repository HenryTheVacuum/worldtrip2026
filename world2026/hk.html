01 The time audit

Two days,
and a real afternoon

This is the easiest arrival on the whole trip. Manila and Hong Kong sit in the same time zone, the flight is barely two hours, and there is no date line and no jet lag to absorb. Take a morning departure out of MNL and you are checked in and walking by early afternoon on Monday — which turns a nominal write-off into something close to a half day. Against that, Thursday's flight to Hanoi eats the last morning. Net: two full days plus a working afternoon, in a city compact enough that this is genuinely enough.

One base, no movingEverything in this dossier is inside a 45-minute radius of Central. There is no second hotel, no bag forwarding, no internal flight. After Japan, Nepal and the Philippines that is a rest in itself — book three nights in one place and never touch the suitcase again.

Arrive on your own termsAirport Express reaches Hong Kong Station in 24 minutes for HK$120 on Octopus. Bus A21 or A11 takes about an hour for roughly a third of that, and the S1 shuttle to Tung Chung plus the MTR is cheaper still. On an arrival day you have already half-salvaged, the train is worth it; on the way out, the bus is fine.

The Monday–Tuesday trapHong Kong's two big West Kowloon museums close on different days, and between them they knock out both your arrival day and your first full day. Section 02 has the fix, but the short version is: West Kowloon happens on Wednesday.

02 Three things to know first

Before you
plan a day

One is unusually good news, one is a scheduling constraint that will quietly ruin a day if you don't route around it, and one is the honest version of what has changed here since most guidebooks were written.

You picked the right week

Best month of the year

The northeast monsoon has taken over by late November and it turns Hong Kong into a different city. Observatory normals for December: mean daily maximum 20.4°C, minimum 16.2°C, 70% humidity, 28.8 mm of rain across 5.3 days and 162 hours of sunshine. November is a few degrees warmer at 24.5°C / 20.3°C, and your window straddles the two — expect low-twenties days and high-teens evenings.

Compare August: 453 mm of rain and a mean of 28.7°C at ninety-odd percent humidity. The real prize is not comfort but visibility — this is the season you can actually see across the harbour and off the ridges, which is the entire point of everything below.

What to pack out of the bagOne light layer for the evening and on the MTR, which is air-conditioned like a meat locker year-round. No umbrella strategy required. Typhoon season is May–November and is effectively finished by the time you land.

The museum calendar is against you

Costs you a day if ignored

M+ is closed Mondays — that's your arrival day, 30 November. The Hong Kong Palace Museum is closed Tuesdays — that's your first full day, 1 December. The two sit four minutes' walk apart in West Kowloon, and there is exactly one day of your stay when both are open: Wednesday 2 December.

Which is also, as it happens, the one night of your stay with racing at Happy Valley — meetings run Wednesday evenings from September to July. Both fit: the museums close at 18:00 and the first race is 18:40 or 19:10, twenty minutes away by MTR.

The fixPin Wednesday: West Kowloon in the afternoon, Happy Valley at night. Everything else in the itinerary floats around that one fixed block.

The honest version

Say it plainly

Global Affairs Canada advises Canadians to exercise a high degree of caution in Hong Kong, citing the risk of arbitrary enforcement of local laws — advice reviewed as recently as 9 September 2026. The national security legislation of 2020 and 2024 is in force and is drawn broadly.

In practice, for a tourist on a three-night stop this means: border officers may inspect the contents of electronic devices, particularly at crossings to the mainland; you must declare cash or bearer instruments over HK$120,000; and political demonstrations and public commentary carry risks they did not a decade ago. None of that changes the ferry, the food or the hills.

Worth knowingA separate Chinese visa is required for the mainland — Hong Kong's visa-free entry does not extend across the boundary. Skip the Shenzhen day trip; on three nights it was never going to fit anyway.

03 The open question

One day out
of the city

Wednesday belongs to West Kowloon and Happy Valley. Monday afternoon and Thursday morning are fragments. That leaves Tuesday 1 December as the only day large enough to leave the urban core — and three genuinely different ways to spend it. All three fit the same window; only one of them is free.

Recommended

The ridge

Shau Kei Wan→ Bus 9 To Tei Wan→ Dragon's Back→ Big Wave Bay→ Shek O

Five-odd kilometres along a spine of hill with the South China Sea on both sides, finishing on a beach with a bus back. Two to three hours at a walking pace, and December is the only season in which this is comfortable rather than an endurance event — the same ridge in August is 33°C at ninety percent humidity.

It is also the single best argument for why this stop sits where it does in the trip. You are two weeks from Cape Town and eight from the Sahara; a cool, dry, high, empty ridgeline three MTR stops from a skyline is not a thing you get twice. Cost: one bus fare.

The Buddha

Tung Chung→ Cable car 25 min→ Ngong Ping→ Tian Tan Buddha→ Tai O

The Ngong Ping 360 cable car runs 5.7 km over water and mountain to a 34-metre bronze Buddha on a plateau, with Po Lin Monastery beside it and the stilt-house village of Tai O reachable by bus afterwards. It is a genuine spectacle and the ride itself is the best part.

It is also the expensive option: HK$295 for a standard-cabin round trip, HK$365 for the glass-floored crystal cabin. Weekday hours are 10:00–18:00, which caps how early you can start. New Lantao Bus route 23 climbs the same mountain road for a small fraction of the fare if you want the destination without the ride.

The island

Central Pier 5→ Ferry 35–60 min→ Cheung Chau→ Mini Great Wall

A dumbbell-shaped fishing island with no cars on it, forty minutes out of Central. Bicycles, a seafood praya, temples, a pirate cave, and a short cliff path called the Mini Great Wall that is a great deal better than the name. Half a day, easily stretched.

The trade-off is that it is restful rather than remarkable. Choose it if eleven countries deep you would rather sit by water than climb something. The fast ferry costs a little more and halves the crossing; the slow one is more pleasant.

Where to put the three nights

Sheung Wan or Central (Hong Kong Island). The recommendation. You are walking distance from Man Mo Temple, Tai Kwun, the Mid-Levels escalator, the tram and the Star Ferry pier, and Airport Express drops you at Hong Kong Station in the middle of it. Sheung Wan is the cheaper, older, more interesting end of the same walk.

Tsim Sha Tsui (Kowloon). Cheaper per night, better for the skyline view — you are looking at Hong Kong Island rather than standing on it — and closer to West Kowloon, Temple Street and Sham Shui Po. Airport Express has its own Kowloon stop at HK$105. A reasonable second choice, especially if the harbour view matters to you.

Not the Peak, not Lantau, not Discovery Bay. Beautiful, and each puts a mountain or a ferry between you and dinner. On three nights, stay where the trains are.

What's cut, whichever Tuesday you pick

Macau. An hour by ferry or bridge bus, a separate immigration crossing, and a full day gone. Portuguese Macau is genuinely worth seeing — and you finish this entire trip in Lisbon eleven weeks from now, which is a strange and pleasing reason to leave it.

Sai Kung and Tai Long Wan. The best beaches and the best coastal hiking in the territory, and both need a full day plus a boat. This is the thing to come back for.

Ocean Park and Disneyland. Both eat a whole day. Neither is why you are here.

Lion Rock and Lantau Peak at sunrise. Superb, and both mean a pre-dawn start on a stop where you finally get to sleep. If the ridge day leaves you wanting more hill, Lion Rock is the one to reach for.

The Star Ferry, and the fare rise sitting behind it

Since April 2023 the Tsim Sha Tsui–Central crossing has cost HK$5 on the upper deck on a weekday (HK$4 lower deck; HK$6.50 and HK$5.60 at weekends). Ten minutes, green and white, wooden benches with reversible backs, and the entire skyline rotating around you. Go at dusk in one direction and after the lights come on in the other — it is worth doing twice on the same evening, which costs you ten dollars.

The operator applied in March 2026 for a further increase of roughly 30% — 80 cents to HK$2 a journey — after years of losses. As of mid-2026 it had not been approved. Even at the proposed fare it stays the cheapest thing in this dossier by an order of magnitude.

Ignore the harbour tour. The same company sells a 45-minute "Water Tour" on the Shining Star at HK$230, or HK$280 for the version timed to the light show. It is the same water. Two ordinary crossings and a bench on the promenade give you more, for two percent of the money.

The Peak: go up cheap, look from the right place

The Peak Tram is a 1888 funicular and worth riding once. Walk-up return is HK$88 on a weekday, HK$108 at weekends; one-way is HK$62. The online Sky Pass bundles the tram return with Sky Terrace 428 for HK$149. Queues at the Garden Road terminus are shortest before ten in the morning and worst between four and seven in the evening, when they can run past an hour.

The move most locals make: bus 15 up from Exchange Square, HK$9.80, forty-odd minutes of switchbacks with better views than the tram tunnel, then walk or take the tram down. And once you are up there, skip the paid deck and walk the Lugard Road circuit — a level, 3.5 km loop around the mountain, free, open all hours, and the source of most of the photographs that make you want to come here.

A Symphony of Lights

Nightly at 20:00, free, roughly ten minutes, and it has been running since 2004. Watch it from the Tsim Sha Tsui promenade — the buildings it choreographs are on the Island side, so Kowloon is where it makes sense. Manage expectations: it is a laser-and-searchlight show over a skyline that was already the spectacle. The skyline is the reason to be standing there; the show is a bonus that arrives on schedule.

What isn't on that list, because it's free

Man Mo Temple. Tai Kwun, the restored Central Police Station compound, including its contemporary art galleries. The Central–Mid-Levels Escalator, 800 metres of it, downhill until 10:00 and uphill after. The Tsim Sha Tsui promenade and the Symphony of Lights. Dragon's Back and every other trail in the territory. The Lugard Road circuit. Chi Lin Nunnery and Nan Lian Garden. Kowloon Walled City Park. Sham Shui Po's street markets. Entry to the public enclosure at Happy Valley, which is HK$10 for residents and free for visitors aged 18 and over on production of a passport.

That list is most of a very good three days. The paid list above is genuinely optional, which is not true of many cities on this route.

Getting aroundThe MTR is fast, cheap, clean and signed in English, and it reaches everything in this dossier. The double-decker trams on Hong Kong Island — the "ding ding" — are a flat HK$3 to anywhere on the line; take the front seat upstairs and ride from Kennedy Town to Causeway Bay for the best-value hour in the city. Minibuses are for the confident.

EatingThe best meals will cost under HK$100 and involve a shared table. Dim sum before eleven, cart-style if you can find it. Wonton noodles, roast goose over rice, claypot rice in Temple Street once the weather is this cool, egg tarts from a bakery counter, and milk tea strong enough to stand a spoon in. Sham Shui Po and Sheung Wan are where to look; hotel restaurants are where not to.

LanguageCantonese and English are both official, and English signage is universal on transport and in museums. Outside the tourist core — Sham Shui Po, the wet markets, a neighbourhood dai pai dong — pointing works fine and is expected.

Typhoons and weather signalsThe season runs roughly May to November and is over by the time you arrive. If a signal ever goes up, the system is public and unambiguous: at Signal 8 the city closes, and that includes the Peak Tram, the ferries and the cable car. In early December the realistic risk is a grey day, not a shut one.
