01 The time audit

Four days
is two days

Dubai to Muscat is seventy-five minutes and no time change at all — both sit on UTC+4, so for the first time since Toronto you arrive already adjusted. That's the good news. The bad news is arithmetic: land Thursday afternoon, fly out Sunday, and what's left is two full days and two fragments, with roughly 600 km of driving threaded through them.

The Friday giftYour one Friday is 8 January, and Friday is the single most consequential day of the Omani week. The Nizwa livestock auction only happens on Friday mornings. The Grand Mosque is the one day it's shut to non-Muslims. The calendar has quietly sorted your itinerary for you: spend Friday inland, save Muscat for Sunday morning.

No public transport worth the nameMwasalat runs a decent coach between Muscat and Nizwa — about two hours, OMR 3, four to six a day. It will not take you to a wadi, a mud village, or a canyon rim. Everything you actually came for is at the end of a road with no bus on it. Budget for a car.

The nights are the constraintThree nights, and Jebel Shams eats one of them. That leaves one for Nizwa and one for Muscat — which is exactly enough if you accept you're seeing Muscat in a single evening and a single morning, and not one minute more.

02 Two things that don't move

Sort one now,
pack for the other

The first is a paperwork problem that has to be solved weeks before you fly and quietly affects four consecutive stops. The second is a temperature problem you solve with a credit card in Muscat.

Your travel insurance may not cover you here

Fix before departure

Three facts that only bite when you put them together. One: Oman's 14-day visa-free entry for Canadians is conditional — the Ministry of Foreign Affairs lists a six-month passport, a return ticket, a confirmed hotel booking, sufficient funds and valid health insurance.

Two: Global Affairs Canada has Oman at "Avoid non-essential travel" — last updated 9 September 2026, citing the volatile regional security situation. The UAE carries the identical wording; Saudi Arabia is the same with named higher-risk areas.

Three: most Canadian travel-medical policies exclude claims arising in a country under a Level 3 or Level 4 Canadian advisory when that advisory was already in force before you left. It has been in force for months. So the default outcome is a policy you paid for that pays nothing — and, on paper, an unmet entry condition.

What to doBuy a policy that names Level 3 destinations as covered, in writing, before you leave Canada — specialist expedition and expat-style insurers write this; mass-market and credit-card policies generally don't. Get the confirmation in an email you can show at a desk. One decision covers stops 19 through 22 — UAE, Oman, Saudi and Egypt — so price it as a four-country problem, not an Oman one. Nobody at Muscat immigration is likely to ask for the certificate; the hospital in Nizwa absolutely will.

The rim camp is a winter camp

Rent the gear, don't skip the night

Camping on the Jebel Shams rim is still free, still legal, still the best thing in the country — none of that has changed. What January changes is the temperature. The rim sits at 3,000 m, and the met station there recorded −1.1°C on 8 January 2024 and −3.6°C in January 2023. The all-time low since monitoring began in 2003 is −9.7°C, also a January.

Daytime is glorious — 5 to 15°C, bone-dry, huge light. It's the eight hours after sunset that matter, and you will be arriving from Colombo and Malé with a bag full of tropical kit and no sleeping bag.

What to doRent in Muscat on the way through. Several outfits hire full camping sets — tent, mat, sub-zero bag, stove — for roughly OMR 10–20 a day, and some will fit a rooftop tent to a 4WD. Book it when you book the car, not when you arrive. The no-tent fallback: Jebel Shams Resort, on the mountain, from about OMR 40 a room — you lose the stars and keep the sunrise.

Nizwa on Friday and Muscat on Sunday are fixed by the opening hours — nothing you decide changes those. The live question is what Saturday is for, and it comes down to whether you spend a night at 3,000 m. Both shapes fit the same 7–10 Jan window and the same one rental car.

Friday still starts at the goat market, then turns uphill to Jebel Akhdar instead — the Saiq plateau at 2,000 m, terraced rose villages stacked on the cliff face, back down to Muscat by evening. Why the recommendation keeps the camp

Wadi Shab is excellent and it is also the fourth-best swimming hole of the last two months — you have come through Kuang Si, El Nido and the Maldives to get here. Jebel Shams has no competition on this itinerary at all. Nothing else between Kathmandu and Cape Town is a 1,000-metre canyon you can sleep on the edge of for nothing.

And the camp is the cheap part. The 4WD is required either way, the Friday is identical either way, and the difference between the two shapes is a OMR 15 gear rental and a night's hotel you don't pay for. If you rent the bag and the night turns out to be miserable, the resort is twenty minutes from the rim.

What's cut in both shapes

The Wahiba Sands. Oman's dune sea, and a proper desert camp. It's the wrong direction from everything else here and needs its own night. You have a Sahara camel night booked in Morocco at stop 26 — let that one carry the dunes.

Sur and the Ras al Jinz turtles. Green turtles nesting on a beach at midnight, three hours-plus each way. It needs a night in Sur and you don't have one.

The Daymaniyat Islands. The best snorkelling in the Gulf of Oman, 42 km offshore, and a full day with a boat. Zanzibar at stop 23 covers this ground better.

Salalah and the Dhofar coast. A different country, effectively — a two-hour flight or ten hours of driving south, and it's at its best in the khareef monsoon between July and September. Not this trip, not this month.

04 The Friday rhythm

What's open
when

Oman's weekend is Friday and Saturday, and the Friday pattern is the reason this stop schedules itself. Two facts carry the whole plan: the Grand Mosque is closed to visitors on Friday, and the Nizwa auction only exists on Friday. Put those side by side and the days fall into place.

The mosque window is three hours wideSaturday to Thursday, 08:00 to 11:00, and that is the whole of it. It is free — an OMR 8.400 admission appeared in January 2025 and was withdrawn after a backlash, so carry OMR 10 in case it comes back. Women need a headscarf and covered arms and legs; men need long trousers and sleeves. Allow 90 minutes inside.

The auction is over by half nineSellers start walking goats into the ring around 06:00, the trading proper runs from about 07:00, and by 09:30 the ring is empty and the fort is where everyone goes next. Free, no ticket, and still overwhelmingly a local event rather than a show. It is the single best argument for sleeping in Nizwa on Thursday night rather than driving out from Muscat at 4am.

Friday afternoon reopensAlmost everything shuts for midday prayers and comes back around 13:30 to 16:00 — Nizwa Fort at 13:30, Mutrah Souq at 16:00. Plan the middle of Friday as driving time and you'll never notice.

07 The mountain

Three thousand
metres of swing

Muscat in January is a pleasant 25°C and barely moves overnight. Jebel Shams is the same country and a different planet: a twenty-degree spread between mid-afternoon and the hour before dawn, and the cold arrives fast once the sun drops behind the western rim.

The road up

Tarmac from Nizwa through Al Hamra and most of the way up the mountain — the climb is steep and switchbacked but sealed, and only the last stretch near the rim viewpoints is graded gravel. People do get saloons up on a dry day. Do not be one of them: the rental contract voids off-tarmac, the gravel is loose on a gradient, and recovery from 3,000 m is not a phone call you want to make on a three-night stop.

Nizwa to the rim is about 90 minutes; Muscat to the rim about 2.5 to 3 hours. There is no fuel, no water, no shop and no toilet on the mountain. Fill the tank in Al Hamra and carry everything, including more water than feels sensible — dry cold dehydrates you without telling you.

The Balcony Walk (W6)

The reason to wake up there. From the trailhead at Al Khitaym the path runs along a ledge cut into the canyon wall, about 6 km out and back, three to four hours, ending at the abandoned cliff village of As Sab with its terraces and falaj channel still visible. It is not technical and it is not a scramble — it is a well-trodden path with a thousand metres of air immediately to one side and no railing anywhere. If exposure bothers you, you will know within the first ten minutes, and turning back is a perfectly good outcome.

Start at first light. In January that means leaving the tent around 07:00 — the canyon is in shadow and cold until the sun clears the far rim, and you want to be walking out, not in, when it gets warm.

Camping, practically

Wild camping is legal across Oman and the rim is the classic spot — flat ground with stone windbreaks already built near the viewpoints, free, no permit, no booking. The convention is to camp away from the road and out of sight of houses, take everything out with you, and not to expect solitude: it's popular and the flat pitches go early on a weekend, which 8 January is.

Pack for a desert night, not a mountain expedition — a three-season bag with a liner, a mat that insulates from the rock, a windproof layer and a hat. The wind matters more than the number on the thermometer. And note the wrinkle: visa-free entry technically wants a confirmed hotel booking, and a tent isn't one. Have the Nizwa and Muscat reservations on your phone and don't volunteer the middle night.

Where the 4WD is actually enforced

Jebel Akhdar. There is a police checkpoint at the foot of the Saiq plateau road and it turns back anything that isn't four-wheel drive. This is a rule, not a recommendation — you will not talk your way past it in a Yaris.

Jebel Shams. No checkpoint, but the final gravel section and your rental agreement both say the same thing. Everywhere else on this itinerary — Muscat, Highway 15 to Nizwa, Bahla, Jabreen, the Sur coast road to Wadi Shab — is fast, empty, beautifully surfaced tarmac that any car handles.

Driving notes

Licence: the Royal Oman Police allow visitors on a tourist entry to drive on a home-country licence for up to three months, and international permits are accepted for the same period. Canada's own travel advice still says to carry an IDP — it costs $25 from CAA, it removes an argument at a rental desk, and one of the two authorities is going to be the one standing in front of you.

Speed: enforcement is by camera and it is relentless. Fines follow the rental company and then follow you. Highway limits are 120 km/h and the cameras are set tight.

Wadis: January brings occasional Mediterranean fronts and wadis flood fast and without much warning. If water is crossing a road, wait or turn around — this is the single most common way visitors die in Oman.

Petrol: stations are frequent on the highways and sparse in the mountains. Fill up in Nizwa or Al Hamra before going up, and again before the run back to Muscat.

WeatherMuscat 24–27°C by day and around 18°C overnight, humidity gone, sea still swimmable at 22–25°C. Nizwa a few degrees warmer by day and noticeably colder at night. January is Oman's peak season for exactly this reason, and it prices accordingly — book rooms well ahead.

Getting in from DXBAround forty-two flights a week: Oman Air, flydubai and Emirates all run it, 75 minutes gate to gate, no time change. An Oman Air late-morning departure puts you on Highway 15 by early afternoon with Nizwa Fort still open at the far end.

Phone and dataOmantel and Ooredoo sell tourist SIMs at MCT arrivals for a few rial. Worth it — you will be navigating unmarked turnings to a mud village, and there is no mobile signal at all on large parts of the Jebel Shams road.
