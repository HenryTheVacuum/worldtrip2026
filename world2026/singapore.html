01 The time audit

Five days
is three days

Singapore Airlines flies SQ441 Kathmandu–Singapore non-stop, daily, about five hours — the only direct on that pair, so the schedule is the schedule. Add the clock: Nepal runs at UTC+5:45, Singapore at UTC+8, so you lose two hours and fifteen minutes to the move. Arrival day and departure day are both fragments. What you actually get is three full days, Tue 17 to Thu 19, and every plan below is built against that number.

The compact-city dividendThree days sounds thin until you remember the country is 50 km end to end and served by one of the best metros on earth. Nothing on this page is more than 45 minutes from anything else on this page. Singapore is the one stop on the whole trip where three days is genuinely enough to see the place rather than sample it.

Changi is not a penaltyThe MRT runs from the airport on the East–West line, no surcharge, roughly 40–50 minutes to the city with one change at Tanah Merah. A taxi is 20–25 minutes. Either way you are checked in and eating within ninety minutes of the belt.

Coming down from altitudeYou arrive from Pokhara and Poon Hill — thin air, cold mornings, hard walking. Singapore is sea level, 31°C and 85% humidity. Your legs will be fine and your thermostat will not. Give day one nothing harder than dinner.

02 The honest bit

You picked
the wet month

There is no way to dress this up: November and December are Singapore's two wettest months, at roughly 255 mm and 330 mm against an annual average nearer 170. November averages about 15 rain days in 30 and the lowest sunshine of the year, around 4.5 hours a day. Statistically, you will get rained on. The argument for coming anyway is not that the forecast is wrong — it's that this is the one city in Asia where rain costs you almost nothing.

What the rain actually does

Plan around it, not through it

Tropical rain here is convective, not frontal — it builds through the late morning, dumps hard for 30 to 90 minutes, and clears. It is not the all-day grey drizzle of a Canadian November. A wet day is usually a dry morning, a loud afternoon and a warm evening.

Two things it genuinely ruins: long open-air walks with no bail-out (the Southern Ridges, MacRitchie's TreeTop Walk, Pulau Ubin) and anything on a beach. Those are the plans to hold loosely and move to whichever morning looks clear.

The leverFront-load. Every outdoor thing on this page belongs before 11:00. Museums, hawker centres, the domes at Gardens by the Bay and the entire underground city exist for the afternoon.

Why it barely matters here

The city is built for it

Singapore is threaded with sheltered walkways, covered five-foot ways under the shophouses, and a genuinely enormous connected underground: you can walk from City Hall to Marina Bay Sands, or across most of Orchard, without going outside. The MRT is air-conditioned end to end.

The best things to eat are all under roofs. Hawker centres, the museums, the two conservatories, Jewel — a rained-out afternoon in Singapore is simply a different good afternoon, which is not true of Bali, Nepal or the Philippines on either side of this stop.

The compensationRain drops the temperature ten degrees and kills the humidity for an hour. The best walking weather you'll get all week is the hour after a storm, usually around dusk.

03 What this stop is for

The service
station

Sixteen days before this you were in Bhutan; five days before this you were walking down from Poon Hill. Ahead of you are Bali, the Philippines and eleven more countries. Singapore is the only stop between Tokyo and Dubai where everything works, everything is in English, and everything is buyable — which makes it worth deliberately spending part of it on admin instead of sights.

GearWhatever broke in Nepal — boots, poles, a pack strap, a dead power bank, a phone screen — gets replaced here properly rather than improvised for the next two months. Sim Lim Square for electronics (haggle, and check the receipt before you leave the counter), Queensway Shopping Centre or Novena for outdoor kit, Mustafa Centre in Little India for the genuinely random, open 24 hours.

LaundryEverything you own has been hand-washed in a teahouse for two weeks. Coin laundries are common in the HDB heartlands and most hotels and hostels have same-day service. Do the whole bag, not the urgent half.

Medical and dentalWorld-class, English-speaking, walk-in, and priced accordingly. If something has been nagging since Kathmandu, this is the best place to deal with it between Tokyo and Cape Town. Travel-insurance paperwork is straightforward here in a way it will not be in Laos.

Onward adminReliable fast wifi and a working printer. Bali's e-VOA and Indonesia's electronic arrival card, the Philippines eTravel form, and anything else that needs a real connection is easier done here than from a Canggu café. Do them on the last afternoon.

The price shockBe ready for it. You arrive from one of the cheapest countries on the itinerary into one of the most expensive. Accommodation and alcohol are the two lines that jump hardest — a beer in a bar is startling, and there is no cheap version. Food is the exception and the great consolation: hawker meals run roughly S$4–8 and are the best value on the entire trip.

Water and the small merciesTap water is potable straight from the tap, everywhere, which will not be true again until Dubai. Refill rather than buy. Public toilets are clean and free. Nothing here will make you ill, which after Nepal and before Indonesia is worth more than it sounds.

04 The one real decision

Where to
put the bag

There is no routing question on this leg — one country, one city, four nights, one base. The only decision that changes the shape of the stop is which neighbourhood you sleep in, because in a city this walkable your doorstep is most of your day. All four below sit on the MRT and none is more than 20 minutes from the others.

Recommended

Chinatown & Tanjong Pagar

The densest concentration of things you actually came to eat. Maxwell Food Centre, Chinatown Complex, Amoy Street and Hong Lim are all inside a ten-minute walk, plus the shophouse bars of Ann Siang Hill and Club Street when you want a roof and a drink.

Why Chinatown wins on a four-night stop

Because your limiting factor here is meals, not sights. You have roughly ten eating opportunities in Singapore and the difference between a good and a great stop is whether you spend them well. Basing within five minutes of Maxwell and Chinatown Complex means breakfast and late supper both happen without a train, which is worth more than a view you'll photograph twice.

The counter-argument is real, though: Kampong Glam is the better-looking neighbourhood to wake up in and the better one to wander at night with nothing planned. If the trip so far has been heavy on logistics and you want four nights of simply being somewhere pleasant, take it and eat one train ride away.

07 The actual reason to come

Ten meals,
spend them well

Singapore's hawker culture is on UNESCO's intangible heritage list and it is, with no serious competition, the best cheap eating on this entire itinerary. A Michelin-recognised plate of food for under S$8 is a normal Tuesday here. Treat the hawker centres as the itinerary and the sights as the gaps between them.

The four that matter, and what they're for

Maxwell Food Centre, Chinatown — the one for a first meal. Central, open-sided, easy to read. Tian Tian Hainanese Chicken Rice is the famous stall and the queue is real; Zhen Zhen porridge and China Street Fritters are the ones locals actually rate. Lunch-heavy, so go at noon rather than at night.

Chinatown Complex — 260-odd stalls over two floors, the largest in the country, and the cheapest. Hong Kong Soya Sauce Chicken Rice & Noodle here was the first street-food stall in the world to hold a Michelin star, at under S$4 a plate. Also the best beer-and-supper scene in the city on the ground floor.

Old Airport Road Food Centre, Geylang East — the connoisseur's pick and the one worth a train ride. 150-plus stalls with the highest density of Michelin-listed hawkers anywhere. Hokkien prawn mee, rojak, satay and barbecued wings after dark. Not central, entirely worth it.

Lau Pa Sat, CBD — a Victorian cast-iron market hall from 1894, which is the draw as much as the food. The reason to come is Satay Street: from around 19:00 the road beside it closes and grills take it over. Touristy, still good, and unbeatable as an evening.

What to order, roughly in order of importance

Hainanese chicken rice — poached chicken, rice cooked in the stock, chilli-ginger on the side. The national dish and the one to eat twice. Chilli crab — the other national dish, and the one exception to the cheap rule: a proper one at East Coast or on the river runs S$70–100 for two, messy, worth doing once. Char kway teow — flat noodles in dark soy and lard, cockles if you're brave. Laksa — coconut curry noodle soup; the Katong version is the one people argue about.

Bak kut teh — peppery pork rib soup, a breakfast dish here. Roti prata with fish curry, and kaya toast with soft eggs and kopi, which is the correct Singaporean breakfast and costs about S$3. Satay at Lau Pa Sat, sold by the stick. Banana-leaf thali or biryani at Tekka in Little India. Nasi lemak and mee goreng from any Malay stall.

How hawker centres work, if you've never used one

Order and pay at the individual stall, not a central till. Take a number or wait; some stalls call, some hand it over. Seating is communal and unassigned — a packet of tissues on a table means it's taken, a local convention called chope that is universally respected and mildly enforced.

Return your tray. This stopped being politeness in 2021 and became law: leaving a dirty tray is a fineable offence, and the trays go to labelled racks by the exits. Most stalls take cards or QR now, but the older ones are cash-only and PayNow doesn't work on a foreign card — carry S$20–30 in small notes.

Stalls close on rotation and many shut one or two days a week with no pattern; the good ones also sell out. If you have a specific stall in mind, check its hours the morning of, and have a second choice in the same building.

08 The transit maths

Skip the
tourist pass

The Singapore Tourist Pass sells unlimited travel at S$17 for one day, S$29 for three and S$45 for five, plus a S$10 refundable card deposit. Against it: any contactless Visa, Mastercard or Amex — or the phone in your pocket — taps straight through the gantry at the ordinary distance-based fare, which runs about S$1.28 to S$2.57 a trip. The three things that actually go wrong

Tap in and tap out with the same card. If you enter on your phone and exit on the physical card, the system can't match the journey and charges the maximum fare on both. This is the single most common tourist mistake and it is not refundable at the gate.

The pass only covers three of your five days. Even on its own terms the S$29 three-day pass leaves your arrival and departure days unpaid, which is the arithmetic above being generous to it. The five-day version at S$45 needs you to make roughly thirty journeys to break even, and you won't.

Grab, not taxis flagged down. Ride-hailing is cheap by Canadian standards, universally used, and priced up front — usually S$8–15 for a cross-town hop. Worth it after a storm, worth it with luggage, unnecessary otherwise. Airport trips carry a surcharge either way.

BusesSame fares, same tap, and often the better ride: the upper deck of a double-decker through Chinatown or down Serangoon Road is a free city tour. Google Maps handles the routing correctly here.

WalkingBetween about 11:00 and 16:00, budget for a slower pace and more stops than you'd think. The heat is not dangerous, but the humidity is relentless and it turns a 25-minute walk into a decision. Everything indoors is aggressively air-conditioned; carry a layer for restaurants and the MRT.

The last dayChangi has free luggage storage counters at reasonable rates and every terminal connects to Jewel, so checking the bag early and going back out is entirely viable. Give yourself three hours before the Bali flight anyway — Changi is efficient but it is enormous.

Weather to pack for31–32°C by day, 24–25°C overnight, humidity above 80%, and rain most days in short bursts. A folding umbrella beats a rain jacket — a shell in this heat is a sauna. Quick-dry everything, sandals you don't mind soaking, and a light layer for the aggressive indoor air-conditioning.

The famous finesReal, but overstated for a visitor behaving normally. Chewing gum can't be sold (bringing a personal amount in is fine); no eating or drinking on the MRT, including water; no smoking outside marked areas; littering and jaywalking are ticketed. Durian is banned on public transport, which you will see signposted and should probably respect.

Language and etiquetteEnglish is an official language and universal. Singlish is a distinct creole and you'll hear "lah", "can/cannot" and "shiok" constantly. Tipping is not customary — restaurants add 10% service and the 9% GST, shown as "++" on menus.

Next stopSIN–DPS is a short hop, about 2h50, with roughly fifteen departures a day across Singapore Airlines, Scoot, Jetstar, Garuda and TransNusa — no shortage of options. Sort Indonesia's e-VOA and electronic arrival card, and Bali's IDR 150,000 tourist levy, from a Singapore desk before you go.
