01 The time audit

Seven days
is four

The itinerary says 8–14 February. Cape Verde doesn't cooperate: nothing leaves Praia in daylight, and the only Monday departure is a six-hour one-stop that has you at the airport on Sunday night. The recommendation already filed against stop 25 is to take Tuesday's 03:50 nonstop instead, landing Casablanca around 09:25 on the 9th. That costs a night's sleep and the whole of the 8th, and leaves four full days and two fragments. Everything below is built against four.

No jet lag, for oncePraia is UTC−1, Morocco is UTC+0. You move the clock forward one hour and that is the entire adjustment — the smallest time change of the whole trip. After Tokyo and Cape Town this stop asks nothing of your body except that you sleep on a plane at four in the morning.

Morocco is on GMT now, permanentlyMorocco abolished its GMT+1 standard and returned to GMT on 20 September 2026, dropping the seasonal switching along with it. That also disposes of the old Ramadan clock shuffle, where the country used to fall back an hour for the month. In February 2027 Morocco is UTC+0, the same as Lisbon, and nothing moves while you're there.

Casablanca is a transit hallCMN is the hub and the Hassan II Mosque is genuinely worth the detour, but Casablanca is not on the see-list and should not get a night. The airport has its own rail station: 45 minutes and 50 MAD to Casa Voyageurs, hourly from 04:00 to 23:00, and every onward train leaves from there.

02 Two things that don't move

Read these
before booking

One reshapes every hour of the stop and cannot be dodged. The other is a routing mistake that is still entirely fixable, and fixing it is worth about four and a half hours of your last day.

The whole stop is inside Ramadan

Unavoidable — and mostly good

Ramadan 1448 is expected to begin on Monday 8 February 2027 — the day this window opens. All seven days sit in Ramadan days 1–7, the most strictly observed stretch of the month. Morocco's Ministry of Habous confirms the start only after the crescent is sighted, so it may slip to the 9th; either way at least six of your days are inside the fast.

What changes: local restaurants shut through daylight, souks close roughly 14:00–17:00, monument hours get clipped (Ben Youssef Madrasa runs 09:00–16:30 in Ramadan against 09:00–19:00 normally), alcohol effectively disappears, and taxis vanish from half an hour before iftar to an hour after. Banks and offices work about 09:00–15:00.

What doesn't: trains, buses, flights and desert tours all run to schedule. Your guides and drivers simply fast while working.

What to doRewrite the shape of the day rather than the dates. Sights and souks in the morning, the dead 14:00–17:00 window spent driving or on a riad terrace, and the evening treated as the main event — Jemaa el-Fna after iftar in Ramadan is the best version of that square there is, and the souks run past midnight. Rooms are reported 10–20% cheaper and the crowds thin out. There is no later window in Westbound that escapes it: Ramadan runs to about 8 March.

Fes ends up with no daylight at all

Fixable — reverse the loop

Written as Casablanca → Marrakech → Sahara → Fes, the desert crossing drops you in Fes at 17:00–18:00 on your second-last day. Departure day then needs Fes → Casa Voyageurs (~4h), a change, and the 45-minute airport shuttle: roughly five hours plus buffer, so you leave Fes around 07:30 for the 15:15 flight. One of the great medinas of this trip gets a dinner and a taxi.

There is no escape hatch. Fes–Lisbon has no nonstop — TAP's last was scheduled 5 January 2020, Ryanair's 27 October 2022, and the route sits inactive.

The fixRun it backwards: Casablanca → Fes → Sahara → Marrakech, and fly out of Marrakech Menara (RAK) instead of Casablanca. RAK–Lisbon has about ten nonstops a week across TAP, Ryanair and easyJet, 1h50. Departure day becomes a 20-minute taxi rather than a five-hour rail relay, Fes gains a full day plus two halves, and the desert crossing ends in the city with the better after-dark life — which is exactly what Ramadan rewards.

03 The open question

Which way
round

Same four bases, same three-day desert crossing, same total driving. The only variable is direction — and direction decides which city gets a real day and how long the last morning lasts. Both shapes below fit a 9 Feb arrival and a 14 Feb departure.

Recommended

Backwards

CMN→ Fes ×2→ Merzouga ×1→ Dades ×1→ Marrakech ×1→ RAK

Land, see the Hassan II Mosque, take the afternoon train to Fes. A full day in the Fes medina — which needs a full day, because it is nine thousand alleys and no cars — then the desert crossing westward, ending in Marrakech on the afternoon of the 13th.

The last morning is real. Jemaa el-Fna, the souks and Ben Youssef before an afternoon flight out of Menara, twenty minutes away. Against the written order that is about four and a half hours handed back.

It also puts the Tizi n'Tichka pass on day 3 inbound. If snow closes it — and it closed twice in January 2026 alone — you lose a detour on the way to a city you're sleeping in, rather than the whole desert trip on day 1.

If the exit has to stay Casablanca

Sometimes the onward ticket is already written that way. If CMN is fixed, you can still run the loop backwards and simply come out of Marrakech by rail: Marrakech → Casa Voyageurs is about 2h40–3h, plus the 45-minute airport shuttle. That is still an hour and a half better than the Fes end, and it keeps Fes's full day.

The alternative rescue for the written order is to sleep in Casablanca on the 13th — train out of Fes that evening, 45 minutes to the airport in the morning. It fixes the departure but still leaves Fes with one evening, and spends a night in a city that isn't on the list.

Or cut Fes. If the desert and Marrakech are the real point, run the crossing as a Marrakech round trip and give Marrakech four days. That removes the transfer problem completely and is a perfectly honest answer on a 27-country trip — Fes is not a place to see badly.

What's cut either way

Chefchaouen. The blue town in the Rif, and it photographs better than anywhere else in Morocco. Four hours north of Fes on a road that leads nowhere else. It needs its own two days.

Essaouira. Three hours west of Marrakech, Atlantic, windy, ramparts and gulls and the best fish on the coast. February is cold and blowing there. It's a day trip you don't have.

Tangier and Al Boraq. Africa's only high-speed line runs Tangier–Kenitra–Rabat–Casablanca at 320 km/h, and it is entirely on the wrong side of your route. The Marrakech extension is a 2030 project.

The Atlas villages. Imlil, the Toubkal valley, Ourika. Reachable from Marrakech in ninety minutes and full of snow in February — a real winter mountain day, but only if you drop something else.

05 The Ramadan clock

A country
on night shift

February is one of the gentlest Ramadans in the 33-year cycle — the fast has drifted into short winter days, so it runs about twelve and a quarter hours rather than the sixteen of a July Ramadan. In Marrakech during your window that means fajr around 05:55 and iftar around 18:15. Fes and Merzouga break fifteen minutes earlier. Learn those two numbers and the whole week makes sense.

The morning is the whole dayMonuments open at nine and thin out fast. Ben Youssef, Bahia, the Saadian Tombs, the Fes madrasas — do them between 09:00 and 13:00 and you will have them close to empty, which almost never happens in Marrakech. Ramadan closing is early: Ben Youssef shuts at 16:30 instead of 19:00, and other sites clip the late afternoon the same way.

The dead hours are a gift, not a lossFrom two to five the souks half-close and the streets empty. Fighting it is pointless. This is when you drive, when you sit on a terrace, when you sleep off the 03:50 flight. In the two shapes above, the long road legs are deliberately parked here.

Do not be in a taxi at sixRoughly thirty minutes before iftar to an hour after, taxis disappear and traffic knots up as everyone races home. Be wherever you intend to break the fast by 17:45. Missing this once will cost you an hour standing on a kerb.

Eat what the month is forIftar opens with dates and harira — the lamb, lentil and tomato soup that only really exists in this form during Ramadan — then chebakia, sticky with honey and sesame, and briouats. Riads and tourist restaurants serve it; so does every stall on the square. It is the best food argument for coming in Ramadan at all.

MannersDon't eat, drink or smoke in the street during daylight — including in the back of the 4×4 while your driver has had nothing since six. Carry water and use it out of sight. Nobody will confront you; everybody will notice.

Alcohol, realisticallyAssume none. Bars, restaurant lists and liquor shops largely stop for the month and supermarkets pause sales. A few international hotels keep serving. If that matters to you, it's a genuine cost of this window — and stop 27 is Lisbon.

07 The crossing

Eighteen hours
in a minibus

The Sahara on this itinerary is not a place you go to; it is a place you drive to, for three days, one way. The standard Marrakech–Fes crossing runs roughly six hours, four hours and eight hours across its three days, with one hour of camel at either end of one night. Going in knowing that number is the difference between a great trip and a grim one.

What €159 actually buys

The shared three-day Marrakech→Fes tour is widely sold at around €159 per person: air-conditioned minibus, hotel pickup, two nights with dinner — a guesthouse in the Dades valley and a Berber tent at Erg Chebbi — the camel trek at sunset and again at sunrise, and showers at Merzouga. Lunches, drinks and tips are not included. The mirrored Fes→Marrakech product is equally standard, though fewer operators list it, which is the one practical reason the reversed loop takes more booking effort.

Shared means a minibus of strangers on someone else's schedule. Private 4×4 costs several times more and buys you departure times, stopping when you want, and a driver who talks to you rather than to the group. On a trip with four full days in the country, that upgrade is more defensible than it sounds — but price it yourself; the figures quoted for private tours vary wildly and none of them are worth repeating here as fact.

Ramadan does not affect the tour. Operators run as scheduled and guides and drivers fast through it — including that eight-hour day.

The pass that closes

Tizi n'Tichka, 2,260 m, is the only road between Marrakech and Ouarzazate, and it "regularly becomes impassable during heavy snowfalls." It was cut twice in January 2026 alone — on the 3rd and again on the 20th. February is squarely in that season.

This is why direction matters more than it looks. Running forwards, Tichka is day 1: a closure on the morning you leave Marrakech can kill the desert leg outright, with no slack in a four-day week to rebuild it. Running backwards, Tichka is the last few hours of day 3, arriving into the city where you're already booked — a delay, not a cancellation. The Fes side climbs the Middle Atlas past Ifrane and Midelt, which carries its own winter risk, but it is not a single choke point.

Ask your operator, in writing, what happens if the pass is shut. A good answer names the Agadir-side detour or an alternative day. No answer is itself an answer.

February in the dunes

Merzouga in February averages about 21°C by day and 8°C at night, with roughly two rainy days and 8 mm across the whole month. Those are averages: clear desert nights run well below the mean, and a tent at Erg Chebbi in February can sit near freezing before dawn. The camps supply heavy blankets. Bring a proper warm layer, a hat and socks to sleep in, and stop thinking of the Sahara as hot.

The upside is enormous. February is dry, the light is long and low all day instead of only at the edges, there is no summer haze, and the dunes are not 45°C. This is the right month to be here, and the itinerary's instinct is correct even where its direction isn't.

How to hold the money

Get dirhams from an airport ATM on arrival. Foreign-card withdrawals carry roughly €3–5 per transaction on top of your own bank's fees, so take out larger amounts less often. Bureau-de-change receipts are what let you convert back at departure; ATM withdrawals don't need documentation.

Carry 20s and 50s. Change for a 200 MAD note is frequently unavailable in the medina, and that becomes your problem, not the vendor's. Cards work in hotels, mid-range restaurants and larger shops; they are useless for petit taxis, street food, souks, hammams and tannery terraces — which is most of what you'll be doing.

Tipping is structural, not optional. Restaurants 10–15%, hotel staff 10–20 MAD a service, taxis rounded to the nearest 5 MAD, and guides 50–100 MAD per person per day — which on a three-day desert crossing is a real line item, not a rounding error.

HammamThe one thing this stop offers that nothing else on the trip does — after a night in a desert tent and eighteen hours in a minibus, a proper scrub is not a spa treatment, it's maintenance. Riads arrange them. Public hammams are cash-only and gendered by time slot; ask which hours are which before you turn up.

The medina, honestlyFes el-Bali is the largest car-free urban area in the world and it is genuinely disorienting — that is the point of it, and no map survives contact. Set a loose destination, accept that you'll arrive somewhere else, and keep your phone's offline map for when you actually need out.

Water and stomachsTap water is chlorinated and locals drink it; visitors on a seven-day stop generally shouldn't gamble. Bottled is cheap everywhere. Fresh orange juice on Jemaa el-Fna is one of the great cheap pleasures of Morocco and is pressed in front of you.

Photographs of peopleAsk first, every time, and expect a request for money on the square — the snake charmers and water sellers on Jemaa el-Fna are working, and a camera pointed at them is a transaction. In Ramadan, add a layer of restraint around anything that looks like someone's private moment at sunset.
