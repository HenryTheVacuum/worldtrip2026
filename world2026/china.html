01 The time audit

Eight days
is six

This is the first stop of the trip where the calendar doesn't lie to you. Seoul to Beijing is a two-hour hop and you gain an hour crossing into UTC+8, so nothing disappears into a date line. Land mid-afternoon on the 29th, fly out mid-morning on the 5th, and you keep six clean days and two useful fragments. After Japan's five, that feels like a windfall. It isn't quite — China spends the difference in other currencies, and the sections below are mostly about those.

No jet lag, no free dawnsJapan handed you 6am for a week. Korea and China are one hour apart, so this time you get nothing — you will have to set an alarm like an adult. That matters more here than it sounds, because China's best hours are still early and its sites are enormous. The Forbidden City is a four-kilometre walk on stone. The Summer Palace is bigger. Budget by distance, not by ticket count.

The two hours you actually loseNot to flying — to friction. Setting up payments, working out that your phone has stopped reaching half the internet, real-name ID checks at every station gate and museum door, and security scanners on the metro. None of it is hard. All of it is slower than Japan, and it lands entirely on day one if you haven't done the prep in Seoul.

Arrival evening is half a day hereYou land around 15:00 with the sun up until nearly 17:30 and no body-clock damage. Unlike Tokyo, you can actually use this evening: get to the hotel, then walk Qianmen and the south end of the central axis in the dark. Don't book anything ticketed — just walk.

02 Three things that don't move

Fixed before
you choose

These hold whichever third city you pick, and two of them need action from you before you leave Korea. The first one is the single biggest structural difference between planning China and planning Japan.

Nothing books more than a week out

Plan from Seoul, not from home

Japan wanted months of notice. China won't take it. The Forbidden City releases tickets exactly seven days ahead, around 20:00 Beijing time, real-name against your passport, no same-day sales, and popular dates go in minutes. The Terracotta Warriors run the same seven-day window against a daily cap. Rail tickets open 15 days out at 08:00 Beijing time and not a minute earlier.

So the trip cannot be locked in before you fly. Your Forbidden City slot for Fri 30 Oct opens on Fri 23 Oct — when you'll be in Seoul. Your Beijing→Xi'an train opens 18 October, mid-Japan.

The fixPut two alarms in your phone now, on Korea time, and treat them as appointments. Everything else on this trip is improvisable; these two are not. Hotels and flights are the only pieces you can genuinely settle in advance.

You don't need a visa

New since February, and temporary

China extended visa-free entry to ordinary Canadian passports on 17 February 2026, for stays up to 30 days, covering tourism and transit. No application, no fee, no appointment — a genuine change, and most travel advice written before last February still says otherwise.

The catch is the expiry. The waiver is declared only to 31 December 2026 and is described as a pilot that could be extended, altered or withdrawn. You land seven weeks before it lapses, which is comfortable but not unlimited — this is a live policy, so re-check it the month you fly.

Still requiredThe free digital arrival card, filed 24–72 hours before you land at the National Immigration Administration portal. Visa-free status does not exempt you. Screenshot the QR code. Any site charging a fee for it is not the government.

Half your phone stops working

Solve it before you land

Google, Gmail, Maps, WhatsApp, Instagram and most Western news sit behind the firewall, and the block lives in the network, not your handset — a local SIM or hotel wi-fi changes nothing. The old answer was a VPN. Through 2026 that stopped being reliable: carriers began cutting VPN-signature traffic on detection, and providers have been degrading or withdrawing their China service.

The answer that currently works is structural rather than clever: a roaming travel eSIM issued outside the mainland routes your data out through its home carrier, so it never meets the filter. Everything simply loads.

Install in SeoulAn eSIM, Alipay with a card bound and passport verified, and Amap or Apple Maps for navigation. All three are materially harder to set up from inside China, which is the trap — the tools you'd use to fix the problem are the ones that are blocked.

03 The open question

Shanghai:
in or out

Beijing and Xi'an pick themselves — the imperial capital and the army, five hours apart on the fastest railway in the world. The third slot is the argument. The default answer is Shanghai, and on a westbound trip the default answer is probably wrong.

Recommended

Chengdu third

Beijing ×4→ Xi'an ×2→ Chengdu ×1→ TFU

The whole week runs one direction — north-east to south-west, Beijing to Xi'an to Chengdu, each leg shorter than the last, and you finish 1,500 km closer to wherever stop 04 is. Beijing keeps four nights, which is what a city with the Wall attached to it actually needs.

What Chengdu adds: the third distinct China of the week. Beijing is imperial and monumental, Xi'an is archaeological, Chengdu is the one where people are sitting in a park drinking tea at two in the afternoon on a Wednesday. Pandas at opening time, and the best food on the route.

Why the recommendation cuts Shanghai rather than Xi'an

Xi'an has a hard floor and a unique object. There is one Terracotta Army, it is two hours round trip from the city plus three hours on site, and the day it sits in cannot be compressed. Beijing at three nights is survivable but it means either the Wall or the Summer Palace goes, and both are load-bearing.

Shanghai, by contrast, is the one city on the list with a strong zero-night case. It is the easiest Chinese city to reach on a future trip, the one most legible without preparation, and the one whose appeal — skyline, colonial architecture, restaurants, galleries — you will meet versions of repeatedly across the remaining 24 stops. Chengdu you will not.

If Shanghai is the trip you want, take it. The two-night version below is real and it works. Just be clear that you're buying it with a Beijing day and a westbound flight.

What's cut either way

Guilin and Yangshuo. The karst river landscape is the most photographed scenery in China and it deserves three days plus a flight in each direction. Not this week.

Suzhou and Hangzhou. Thirty minutes and fifty minutes from Shanghai by train, and Suzhou's classical gardens hit late-autumn peak in exactly your window. They only exist as an option in the Shanghai shape, and only by giving up the Shanghai day itself.

Mount Hua. Ninety minutes from Xi'an, with the plank walk everyone has seen. A full day and a hard one — it would eat the Terracotta day.

Hong Kong. Separate immigration, separate currency, separate trip. It's also the obvious pairing for a future Shanghai visit.

06 The colour question

This time
you're on time

Japan's gardens were going to be entirely green three weeks early. North China runs on a different clock, and your window lands almost exactly on it. Beijing's colour arrives from the mountains inward through late October and reaches the city parks in early November — Fragrant Hills peaks in the days you are there, and the ginkgo avenues turn as you leave. This is the compensation for missing koyo, and it is a real one.

Peak colourYour window · indicative averages, colour shifts a week either way each year

The one thing to time precisely

Fragrant Hills, before nine, on a weekday. Xiangshan is an 800-year-old imperial hunting park with something over a hundred thousand sumac and maple on the hillside, and it is the definitive Beijing autumn image. It is also mobbed — the Red Leaf Festival has drawn well over 800,000 people in a season, concentrated into about three weekends, which are your weekends.

Go on Friday 30 October at opening. Take the south trails from the east gate rather than fighting up Incense Burner Peak with everybody else; the colour is concentrated on the southern slopes anyway, the views are wider, and the crowd thins within twenty minutes of walking. Be off the hill by early afternoon — the east-facing slopes lose their light after two.

And the air, which is the other seasonal variable

Beijing's air is at its best in autumn and its worst in deep winter, and the hinge is the municipal heating season, which conventionally starts around 15 November. You are ten days ahead of it. Late October and early November also get reliable north-westerly winds that scour the basin clear, which is why the classic long-lens shots of the Forbidden City against the Western Hills are autumn photographs.

This is odds, not a guarantee — a still, warm week can go grey regardless. But you have picked the right fortnight, and the practical advice is simply to keep the Wall day and the Fragrant Hills day flexible by a day if you can, and spend the clearest morning on whichever of them is outstanding.

07 The Wall

Go further
than you think

There are a dozen visitable sections within a few hours of Beijing and they are not interchangeable. The standard advice is Mutianyu, and on a weekday the standard advice is right. Your only available Wall day is Saturday 31 October, in the middle of Beijing's peak leaf-viewing fortnight, which changes the answer.

Badaling

No

The closest section, thirty minutes from Beijing North by high-speed train, fully restored, wide enough to walk ten abreast — and around ten million visitors a year, with peak days approaching eighty thousand people at once. Coach parties arrive in waves from nine.

It is the right choice for exactly one person: someone with mobility limits or half a day, who needs the Wall to be easy. That isn't you.

Mutianyu

The weekday answer

Seventy kilometres out, 90 minutes to two hours by road, well-restored Ming wall along a forested ridge with 23 towers, a cable car up and a metal toboggan down. Entry is modest; the chairlift-plus-toboggan combination runs a couple of hundred yuan and is worth it.

Footfall runs well under half of Badaling's. On a Tuesday it is close to ideal. On a Saturday in leaf season it is a queue, and the cable car is where the queue forms.

If you take it anywayLeave central Beijing by 07:00, walk up rather than queueing for the lift, and head left towards the steeper western towers where the crowd thins fast.

Jinshanling

The Saturday answer

130 km out, two to two and a half hours, and the reason to make the drive: ten kilometres of ridgeline wall running through partly-restored and partly-wild towers, which is the version in every photograph you actually admire. Crowds are a fraction of Mutianyu's, and the extra hour of travel is what buys that.

It's a real walk — three to four hours on uneven stone with genuine ascent — and there's a cable car if you'd rather start high. Late October leaves it in brown-and-gold hill country with the towers stepping away to the horizon.

The tradeYou spend five hours in a car to buy solitude on a Saturday. On this particular Saturday that is the right trade, and it's the recommendation in the day-by-day above.

Two others, for completeness

Simatai and Gubei Water Town — the only section with a regular night programme, illuminated, with numbers capped and advance booking required. It works as an overnight rather than a day trip, and the water town beneath it is a modern reconstruction that people either enjoy or resent. Not on a seven-night week.

Jiankou — unrestored, collapsing, spectacular, and genuinely dangerous in places. For experienced hikers with a guide, and its colour peaks in mid-October, a fortnight before you arrive.

Whichever you choose, book the car or the tour a few days out rather than on the morning. Transport to the non-Badaling sections is the part that sells out, not the Wall.

The opposite verdict to Japan's. There is no pass to evaluate here — China has no tourist rail pass and doesn't need one, because point-to-point fares are low. The only real question on each leg is train versus plane, and the honest comparison is door to door: stations sit in city centres, airports do not. Tick the legs you'll ride.

How to actually buy them

Tickets go on sale 15 days before departure at 08:00 Beijing time and there is no way to buy earlier. Use the official Railway 12306 app, which has an English interface, accepts registration on a foreign passport with no Chinese phone number, and charges no booking fee. Trip.com is easier to set up and adds a small fee; it's the sensible fallback if 12306's payment step rejects your card.

Everything is real-name and e-ticketed. Your passport is the ticket — lay it on the reader at the gate and walk through. Nothing to print. If a smaller station's automatic lane doesn't list foreign passports, the staffed lane beside it does the same job by hand.

Arrive 30 minutes early at ordinary stations and 45 at the giants — Beijing West and Shanghai Hongqiao are airport-sized, with airport-sized security queues. Confirm which station you're leaving from when you book; Beijing has several and they are not near each other.

Which class

Second class on a G-train is comfortable, quiet, has a power socket and is what almost everyone rides. First class buys a slightly wider seat in a slightly calmer carriage for roughly sixty percent more. On a five-hour Beijing→Xi'an run it's a reasonable indulgence; on the three-hour Xi'an→Chengdu leg it isn't. Business class is a flat-bed product at close to airfare and makes no sense on these distances.

WeatherBeijing runs about 14–17°C by day and 3–6°C at night in your window, dry and often bright, with the first genuinely cold mornings arriving in early November. Xi'an is a degree or two milder, Chengdu warmer, greyer and damper — the Sichuan basin sits under cloud most of the year. Pack a real jacket for Beijing dawns; you'll be outdoors before sunrise twice.

NavigationGoogle Maps does not work. Amap has an English mode and is the one locals use; Apple Maps works and is adequate. Screenshot your hotel's name and address in Chinese characters before you need it — taxi drivers won't read pinyin, and DiDi's English app is the easier route anyway.

ID checksCarry your passport at all times, not a copy. It's your train ticket, your museum ticket, your hotel check-in and your metro-security fallback. Hotels register you with the local police automatically; that's routine, not a problem.

EatingAs in Japan, the best meals will be cheap and fast — Xi'an's roujiamo and biangbiang noodles, Chengdu's breakfast noodles, a Beijing hutong dumpling counter. Reserve one Peking duck dinner and one Chengdu hotpot and improvise the rest. Point at what other people are eating; it works.
