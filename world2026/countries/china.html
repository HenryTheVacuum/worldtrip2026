01 The time audit

Eight days
is six

This is the first stop of the trip where the calendar doesn't lie to you. Seoul to Beijing is a two-hour hop and you gain an hour crossing into UTC+8, so nothing disappears into a date line. Land mid-afternoon on the 29th, fly out mid-morning on the 5th, and you keep six clean days and two useful fragments. With only one base change in the middle of it, this is the least logistically fraught week between Toronto and Kathmandu.

No jet lag, no free dawnsJapan handed you 6am for a week. Korea and China are one hour apart, so this time you get nothing — you'll have to set an alarm like an adult. That matters more than it sounds, because China's best hours are still early and its sites are enormous. The Forbidden City is a four-kilometre walk on stone. The Summer Palace is bigger. Budget by distance, not by ticket count.

The two hours you actually loseNot to flying — to friction. Setting up payments, working out that your phone has stopped reaching half the internet, real-name ID checks at every station gate and museum door, and security scanners on the metro. None of it is hard. All of it is slower than Japan, and it lands entirely on day one if you haven't done the prep in Seoul.

Arrival evening is half a day hereYou land around 15:00 with light until nearly 17:30 and no body-clock damage. Unlike Tokyo, you can use this evening: get to the hotel, then walk Qianmen and the south end of the central axis in the dark. Don't book anything ticketed — just walk.

02 Three things that don't move

Fixed before
you choose

These hold whichever way the nights are split, and two of them need action from you before you leave Korea. The first is the single biggest structural difference between planning China and planning Japan.

Nothing books more than a week out

Plan from Seoul, not from home

Japan wanted months of notice. China won't take it. The Forbidden City releases tickets exactly seven days ahead, around 20:00 Beijing time, real-name against your passport, no same-day sales, and popular dates go in minutes. Rail tickets open 15 days out at 08:00 Beijing time and not a minute earlier.

So the trip cannot be locked in before you fly. Your Forbidden City slot for Fri 30 Oct opens on Fri 23 Oct — when you'll be in Seoul. The Beijing→Shanghai train for Mon 2 Nov opens Sun 18 Oct, mid-Japan.

The fixPut two alarms in your phone now, on Korea and Japan time, and treat them as appointments. Hotels and flights are the only pieces you can genuinely settle in advance; everything else on this stop is improvisable except these.

You don't need a visa

New since February, and temporary

China extended visa-free entry to ordinary Canadian passports on 17 February 2026, for stays up to 30 days, covering tourism and transit. No application, no fee, no appointment — a genuine change, and most travel advice written before last February still says otherwise.

The catch is the expiry. The waiver is declared only to 31 December 2026 and is described as a pilot that could be extended, altered or withdrawn. You land seven weeks before it lapses, which is comfortable but not unlimited — re-check it the month you fly.

Still requiredThe free digital arrival card, filed 24–72 hours before you land, through the National Immigration Administration portal. Visa-free status does not exempt you. Screenshot the QR code. Any site charging a fee for it is not the government.

Half your phone stops working

Solve it before you land

Google, Gmail, Maps, WhatsApp, Instagram and most Western news sit behind the firewall, and the block lives in the network, not your handset — a local SIM or hotel wi-fi changes nothing. The old answer was a VPN. Through 2026 that stopped being reliable: carriers began cutting VPN-signature traffic on detection, and providers have been degrading or withdrawing their China service.

The answer that currently works is structural rather than clever: a roaming travel eSIM issued outside the mainland routes your data out through its home carrier, so it never meets the filter. Everything simply loads.

Install in SeoulAn eSIM, Alipay with a card bound and passport verified, and Amap or Apple Maps for navigation. All three are materially harder to set up from inside China — the tools you'd use to fix the problem are the ones that are blocked.

03 The open question

Where the
seventh night goes

Two cities, seven nights, one train between them — so the only structural decision left is the split, and it comes down to whether Beijing gets a fourth day or Shanghai gets a fourth night. Both shapes below move on the same 4h30 train and fly out of the same airport.

Recommended

Beijing 4 / Shanghai 3

Beijing ×4→ Shanghai ×3→ Suzhou day→ PVG

Beijing has four things that each want most of a day — the Forbidden City, the Wall, the Summer Palace, and Fragrant Hills at peak colour — and three nights forces one of them out. Four nights fits all of it without a single compressed morning.

Shanghai still gets what it needs. An arrival afternoon in the Concession and the Bund at night, one full city day, and a Suzhou day trip thirty minutes down the line. Shanghai is a walking-and-eating city; it rewards three unhurried days more than four hurried ones.

What cutting Xi'an costs, stated plainly

This stop previously ran Beijing → Xi'an → Chengdu. Dropping to two cities removes one thing that has no substitute anywhere else on the itinerary: the Terracotta Army. Eight thousand individually modelled figures, still being excavated, and there is no second-best version of it in another country on this route.

What you get back is real and it isn't small. The week goes from two base changes to one, from eight rail hours to four and a half, and from three half-unpacked hotel rooms to two. Xi'an at two nights was always the tightest stop of the three — one day out at the pits, one evening in the Muslim Quarter, and a train at each end — and a rushed Terracotta Army is a worse outcome than a deliberate absence.

It is also the most reachable thing you're giving up. Xi'an is two visa-free hours from Shanghai by air and four and a half by rail; it is a long weekend from anywhere in China. Hegra, Ajanta and Jinshanling are not. If the trade bothers you, the honest fix is a future week in Xi'an and Chengdu together, not a compressed day inserted here.

What's cut either way

Hangzhou, unless the Shanghai-heavy shape is chosen. West Lake at fifty minutes is the obvious second Jiangnan day and it only exists in the second column above.

Guilin and Yangshuo. The karst river landscape is the most photographed scenery in China and it needs three days plus a flight each way.

Hong Kong. Separate immigration, separate currency, separate trip — and the natural pairing for a future Shanghai visit rather than a bolt-on to this one.

A second Wall section. One Wall day is what this week affords. §07 is about spending it correctly rather than twice.

06 The colour question

North yes,
south early

Japan's gardens were going to be entirely green three weeks early. North China runs on a different clock and your window lands almost exactly on it — Fragrant Hills peaks in the days you're in Beijing, and the palace ginkgo turns as you leave for the train. Shanghai is the other way round: its plane trees don't go until mid-November. Suzhou, luckily, splits the difference and is in season.

The one thing to time precisely

Fragrant Hills, before nine, on a weekday. Xiangshan is an 800-year-old imperial hunting park with something over a hundred thousand sumac and maple on the hillside, and it is the definitive Beijing autumn image. It is also mobbed — the Red Leaf Festival has drawn well over 800,000 people in a season, concentrated into about three weekends, which are your weekends.

Go on Friday 30 October at opening. Take the south trails from the east gate rather than fighting up Incense Burner Peak with everybody else; the colour is concentrated on the southern slopes anyway, the views are wider, and the crowd thins within twenty minutes of walking. Be off the hill by early afternoon — the east-facing slopes lose their light after two.

And the air, which is the other seasonal variable

Beijing's air is at its best in autumn and its worst in deep winter, and the hinge is the municipal heating season, which conventionally starts around 15 November. You are ten days ahead of it. Late October and early November also get reliable north-westerly winds that scour the basin clear, which is why the classic long-lens shots of the Forbidden City against the Western Hills are autumn photographs.

Shanghai doesn't have this problem in the same way — it is coastal, milder and damper, running 17–20°C by day in early November against Beijing's 14–17°C, and its seasonal risk is drizzle rather than haze. Pack the warm layer for Beijing dawns and something that sheds rain for the Jiangnan days.

07 The Wall

Go further
than you think

There are a dozen visitable sections within a few hours of Beijing and they are not interchangeable. The standard advice is Mutianyu, and on a weekday the standard advice is right. Your only available Wall day is Saturday 31 October, in the middle of Beijing's peak leaf-viewing fortnight, which changes the answer.

Badaling

No

The closest section, thirty minutes from Beijing North by high-speed train, fully restored, wide enough to walk ten abreast — and around ten million visitors a year, with peak days approaching eighty thousand people at once. Coach parties arrive in waves from nine.

It is the right choice for exactly one person: someone with mobility limits or half a day, who needs the Wall to be easy. That isn't you.

Mutianyu

The weekday answer

Seventy kilometres out, 90 minutes to two hours by road, well-restored Ming wall along a forested ridge with 23 towers, a cable car up and a metal toboggan down. Entry is modest; the chairlift-plus-toboggan combination runs a couple of hundred yuan and is worth it.

Footfall runs well under half of Badaling's. On a Tuesday it is close to ideal. On a Saturday in leaf season it is a queue, and the cable car is where the queue forms.

If you take it anywayLeave central Beijing by 07:00, walk up rather than queueing for the lift, and head left towards the steeper western towers where the crowd thins fast.

Jinshanling

The Saturday answer

130 km out, two to two and a half hours, and the reason to make the drive: ten kilometres of ridgeline wall running through partly-restored and partly-wild towers, which is the version in every photograph you actually admire. Crowds are a fraction of Mutianyu's, and the extra hour of travel is what buys that.

It's a real walk — three to four hours on uneven stone with genuine ascent — and there's a cable car if you'd rather start high. Late October leaves it in brown-and-gold hill country with the towers stepping away to the horizon.

The tradeYou spend five hours in a car to buy solitude on a Saturday. On this particular Saturday that's the right trade, and it's the recommendation in the day-by-day above.

Two others, for completeness

Simatai and Gubei Water Town — the only section with a regular illuminated night programme, capped and booked ahead. It works as an overnight rather than a day trip, and the water town beneath it is a modern reconstruction that people either enjoy or resent. Not on a seven-night week with one base change already in it.

Jiankou — unrestored, collapsing, spectacular, and genuinely dangerous in places. For experienced hikers with a guide, and its colour peaks in mid-October, a fortnight before you arrive.

Whichever you choose, book the car or the tour a few days out rather than on the morning. Transport to the non-Badaling sections is the part that sells out, not the Wall.

08 The rail maths

Take the
train

The opposite verdict to Japan's, and with two cities there is now exactly one intercity decision to make. Beijing to Shanghai is 1,318 km — the flagship line, up to 350 km/h, four and a half hours station to station. Against that, flying is two and a quarter hours in the air and roughly three more in airports. Pick a mode and watch the door-to-door number, which is the only one that matters.

The day trips are trivially cheap

Shanghai → Suzhou runs about 25–40 minutes with departures every few minutes, roughly ¥40 each way second class, from Hongqiao to Suzhou North. Shanghai → Hangzhou East is 50–65 minutes at roughly ¥73 each way. Neither needs planning beyond turning up 30 minutes early, and neither has a flight worth considering.

Both still go on sale 15 days out and both sell out on weekends. Wednesday 4 November is a weekday, which is the main reason Suzhou sits there in the recommended shape.

How to actually buy them

Tickets go on sale 15 days before departure at 08:00 Beijing time and there is no way to buy earlier. Use the official Railway 12306 app, which has an English interface, accepts registration on a foreign passport with no Chinese phone number, and charges no booking fee. Trip.com is easier to set up and adds a small fee; it's the sensible fallback if 12306's payment step rejects your card.

Everything is real-name and e-ticketed. Your passport is the ticket — lay it on the reader at the gate and walk through. Nothing to print. If a smaller station's automatic lane doesn't list foreign passports, the staffed lane beside it does the same job by hand.

Arrive 30 minutes early at ordinary stations and 45 at the giants — Beijing South and Shanghai Hongqiao are airport-sized, with airport-sized security queues. Confirm which station you're leaving from when you book; Beijing has several and they are not near each other. The Shanghai trains go from Beijing South, not Beijing West.

WeatherBeijing runs about 14–17°C by day and 3–6°C at night, dry and often bright, with the first genuinely cold mornings arriving in early November. Shanghai is warmer, damper and greyer — 17–20°C by day, and drizzle is the likelier disruption. Pack a real jacket for the Beijing dawns; you'll be outdoors before sunrise twice.

NavigationGoogle Maps does not work. Amap has an English mode and is the one locals use; Apple Maps works and is adequate. Screenshot your hotel's name and address in Chinese characters before you need it — taxi drivers won't read pinyin, and DiDi's English app is the easier route anyway.

ID checksCarry your passport at all times, not a copy. It's your train ticket, your museum ticket, your hotel check-in and your metro-security fallback. Hotels register you with the local police automatically; that's routine, not a problem.

EatingThe two cities eat nothing alike — Beijing is wheat, lamb, vinegar and duck; Shanghai is sweeter, lighter and river-fed. Reserve one Peking duck dinner and one proper Jiangnan meal, and improvise everything else at counters. Point at what other people are eating; it works.
