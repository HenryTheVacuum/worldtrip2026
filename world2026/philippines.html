01 The time audit

Six days,
and two of them
are corridors

Bali to Manila is a four-hour hop with no time change at all — Denpasar and Manila both sit on UTC+8, so for the first time on this trip you arrive without losing anything to a clock. That gift gets taken straight back by Palawan. Getting to El Nido and back now costs the better part of two days, which turns a six-day stop into four usable days and one full day of island-hopping.

No jet lag, for onceBali is WITA, UTC+8. Manila is UTC+8. You land on the same clock you left on — the only stop on this entire itinerary where that's true. Cebu Pacific and Philippine Airlines both fly DPS–MNL non-stop, around 17 departures a week between them, roughly 4h20 in the air.

Arrival day is a half dayWhatever the schedule says, NAIA plus Manila traffic will eat the rest of it. Grab from the airport to Makati or Malate is thirty minutes at 05:00 and ninety at 18:00, and there is no version where you land and also see something.

You leave on a holidayMonday 30 November is Bonifacio Day, a regular national holiday, and it caps a long weekend. Your Hong Kong flight is unaffected — the route runs about 86 times a week — but domestic terminals, roads and ferries will be at their fullest on the way out.

02 The thing that changed

Manila lost
Palawan

This leg was planned around a one-hour flight from Manila to El Nido's own little airport. That flight no longer exists. Cebu Pacific bought AirSWIFT in October 2024 and retired the brand into its regional arm Cebgo on 1 July 2026; in the reshuffle, Manila came off the El Nido route map — and off Coron's too. Verify this against Cebu Pacific directly before you rebuild the leg, but two independent route databases now agree.

MNL → El Nido: gone

Route terminated

El Nido's Lio Airport (ENI) is now served by Cebgo alone, non-stop from Clark, Cebu, Caticlan, Busuanga and Tagbilaran — and from nowhere else. Manila appears on the map only as a connection.

The old service was the single best thing about visiting El Nido: an hour and ten minutes from the capital onto a 72-seat ATR, landing four kilometres from town. Reaching the same beach now takes six to ten hours.

What survivesClark inherited the traffic. CRK–ENI is now the busiest route into El Nido at roughly 112 flights a month — three to four a day — and Clark is two to three hours north of Manila by bus.

Coron is not the escape hatch

Same problem

The obvious fix — swap El Nido for Coron, which has better wreck diving anyway — doesn't work. Busuanga (USU) also lost its Manila flights in the same period, and now flies only to Clark, Cebu and El Nido.

Northern Palawan as a whole has been re-plumbed through Clark. If you want the karst-and-lagoon version of the Philippines out of Manila, there is no longer a short way to get it.

The one exceptionPuerto Princesa (PPS), in central Palawan, is untouched — about 42 flights a week from Manila on Cebu Pacific and PAL, 1h40 in the air. It is 240 km south of El Nido, and that is the catch.

Recommended

Via Clark

Manila ×2→ Bus CRK→ Fly ENI→ El Nido ×2→ Manila ×1

Genesis runs a P2P coach from NAIA Terminal 3 to Clark every hour, around the clock, for ₱450 — 24 departures a day. Two to three hours depending on traffic, then a one-hour Cebgo hop to Lio and a ₱150–200 tricycle into town.

The trick is the overnight bus. Because the P2P runs through the night, you can take a 03:00 or 04:00 departure from NAIA, clear Clark in the quiet hours and be on the first flight out — which is what makes this shape land you in El Nido before lunch instead of after it.

The risk is the bus. One traffic jam on NLEX and you miss a flight that only goes three times a day. Build two hours of slack you expect to waste.

Slower, steadier

Via Puerto Princesa

Manila ×2→ Fly PPS→ Van 5½h→ El Nido ×2→ Manila ×1

Everything happens at one airport you're already at. Cebu Pacific and PAL fly MNL–PPS about six times a day; from Puerto Princesa, Lexxus Shuttle runs air-conditioned vans north seven times daily, 07:00 to 17:00, for ₱650, and the trip takes about five and a half hours.

Nothing here can strand you the way a missed Cebgo flight can — miss a van and there's another one. But it is a genuinely long day, most of it on the Palawan Highway, and it costs you roughly three and a half hours more than the Clark shape in each direction.

The consolation: the Puerto Princesa Underground River is on this side of the island. It is also a full day, which you do not have.

The honest option

Cut Palawan

Manila ×5→ Corregidor→ Taal→ HKG

Two travel days buy one day on a boat. Spend them in Luzon instead and you get four full days: Intramuros properly rather than in a dawn sprint, the Corregidor ferry, a Taal volcano day out of Tagaytay, and Binondo on an evening when you aren't packing.

This is a worse holiday and a better use of six days. On a 27-country trip where Indonesia just gave you beaches and Thailand gave you boats, El Nido's marginal value is lower than it looks on the map.

If you cut it, cut it now. El Nido rooms over the Bonifacio long weekend are the expensive thing to cancel late.

Why Clark wins on paper and might not in practice

Clark is three and a half hours faster each way, which on a stop this short is most of an afternoon. But the two shapes fail differently. The Puerto Princesa route fails slowly — a delayed flight means a later van, and there's always a later van. The Clark route fails hard: the P2P bus is the only sensible link, it runs through the worst road corridor in the country, and CRK–ENI departs three or four times a day. There is no recovery on the same day.

Take Clark if you'll actually get on a 03:00 bus. Take Puerto Princesa if you won't. Both are defensible; the one that isn't is booking Clark and then leaving Makati at seven in the morning.

The greedy variant, and what it costs you

The day-by-day below returns to Manila on Sunday 29 November and sleeps near the airport. You can instead stay a second full day in El Nido and fly out on the morning of the 30th — ENI to Clark around 08:00, the P2P south, and an evening MNL–HKG departure (the route runs as late as 21:10). That buys you a second island-hopping day, which is the difference between seeing Tour A and seeing Tour A and Tour C.

It also puts an international connection behind a domestic turboprop, a shuttle bus and a public holiday's traffic, on a day with no slack whatsoever. If a morning flight out of Lio cancels for weather — which happens — you miss Hong Kong. Only do this if the Hong Kong ticket is cheap enough to lose.

What's cut in every version

Coron. The wrecks of the Japanese fleet sunk in 1944, Kayangan Lake, Barracuda Lake. No Manila flight any more, and the El Nido–Coron fast ferry is four to five hours each way. Needs its own trip.

Cebu, Bohol and Siargao. All perfectly reachable from Manila, all a separate week. The Philippines does not reward a five-night visit and this one is honestly a taster.

Banaue and the rice terraces. Nine hours north by night bus. The single most spectacular thing in the country that isn't underwater, and completely out of reach here.

05 What's actually worth doing

Do, maybe,
don't bother

Manila is a city most itineraries treat as an airport with a hotel attached. That's wrong, but it is a city that hides its good parts, so the do list here is short and specific. El Nido's entries are mostly about which boat you get on.

06 The El Nido bill

The ₱1,500
boat trip

El Nido's advertised tour prices are real, and they are also not what you pay. On top of the boat sits a municipal eco-tourism fee, a park fee, a town tourism fee, and a short queue of small extras handed over at the jetty in cash. None of it is a scam — the fees fund reef and mangrove work — but it roughly doubles a day on the water, and nobody tells you until you're standing there. Tick what applies.

Bring the cash from Manila

Almost all of the above is cash only, in pesos. El Nido has a small number of ATMs, they run dry, they cap withdrawals low, and they charge per transaction — the single most reliable complaint about the town. Draw what you need in Manila, where 7-Eleven and bank ATMs are everywhere, and carry it.

GCash covers a lot of the country now, but it wants a Philippine mobile number to set up. If you're buying a local SIM anyway it's worth ten minutes at the airport counter; if not, don't plan around it.

Which tour, if you only get one

Tour A is the one in every photograph — Big Lagoon, Small Lagoon, Secret Lagoon, Shimizu Island, Seven Commandos Beach. It is also the busiest, and the Small Lagoon usually means an extra ₱150–200 for a kayak. If you have one day and want the picture you came for, take it.

Tour C is the better day out: Hidden Beach through a cave passage, Matinloc Shrine, Talisay, Secret Beach, Tapiutan Strait. Longer boat ride, rougher water, more dramatic limestone, fewer people. In late November the sea can be lively enough that operators cancel it — ask the night before, not the morning of.

Tours B and D are the quiet ones and the right answer on a second or third day, not a first. Nobody's first day in El Nido should be Tour D.

07 The weather, honestly

Manila is dry.
El Nido
is not yet.

The plan says the typhoon season is winding down and the amihan north-easterlies are arriving, and that's true — for most of the country. Palawan runs about a month behind. Manila in November averages 76 mm of rain across 7 wet days; El Nido averages 222 mm across 17. El Nido's dry season doesn't properly arrive until January.

What 17 rain days actually meansNot seventeen washed-out days. Late-November rain in Palawan is mostly overnight and early-morning convective stuff that clears — the pattern that makes island-hopping viable is a wet dawn and a bright eleven o'clock. What it does mean is that a single-day plan has real exposure: if your one day is the bad one, there is no second day.

TyphoonsPalawan sits south and west of the main corridor and is hit far less often than Luzon or the Visayas — but "rarely" is not "never", and Haiyan crossed northern Palawan in 2013. PAGASA puts about 70% of the season in July–October, so late November is genuinely the shoulder. Watch the forecast in the week before, not the month.

Sea stateThe amihan that dries the country also puts wind on the water. From late November the north-east swell starts to build on exposed coasts, and Tour C — the long run out to Tapiutan — is the first thing operators drop. Tours A and D sit in more sheltered water.

HeatAround 29°C in Manila and 28–29°C in El Nido, with sea temperature 26–29°C. Humid but not the punishing version — you're two months past the worst of it, and Manila in particular is at its most walkable of the year.

TrafficGrab is the default and works well; surge pricing at rush hour is brutal and the app will quote it honestly. Metro Manila's rush hours are roughly 07:00–10:00 and 16:00–20:00, and EDSA is worse than whatever you're picturing. Budget double what a map says, always.

EatingBinondo is the oldest Chinatown in the world, founded in 1594, and it is the best eating in Manila — kiampong, lumpia, fried siopao, hokkien noodles, bakeries that have been there for generations. Do it on foot, in the late afternoon, hungry. In El Nido, eat where the boats land.

LanguageEnglish is an official language and is spoken more widely and more fluently here than anywhere else on this trip. After Japan, Korea, China and Indonesia, this is the stop where you can just ask.

Boats and sea stateIsland-hopping runs on bancas — outrigger boats, no shade to speak of, no toilet on most. Dry bag, reef-safe sunscreen, a hat you don't mind losing. Operators cancel for weather and are right to; if they cancel, they cancel.
