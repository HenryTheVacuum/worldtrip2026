01 The time audit

Seven days,
and you get them

For the first time in twenty-six stops, the arithmetic is on your side. Casablanca to Lisbon is ninety minutes with no clock change — Morocco moved permanently to GMT on 20 September 2026, so Morocco, Portugal and the UK all read the same time in February. No date line, no jet lag, no lost day. Take a morning departure and 14 February is a real half-day, not a write-off.

Take the morning flightRoyal Air Maroc's daily CMN–LIS is AT982 at 15:20 — that hands you a 17:00 arrival and nothing else. TAP's TP1437 leaves Casablanca 10:25 on Wednesdays, Saturdays and Sundays, which 14 February is. Same fare bracket, four extra hours in Lisbon. If the Morocco reversal holds and you're flying out of Marrakech instead, TP1453 at 10:45 does the same job on ten weekly nonstops.

The one entry that countsPortugal is the only Schengen country on the whole trip. Your 90-in-180 clock starts at zero on arrival and you use seven of them. Nothing from Japan onward touches it. The complication is not the allowance — it's the machinery at the border, which is section 02.

Decompression is a legitimate use of a dayTwenty-six countries in four months ends here. Lisbon is a city where doing nothing well is the local competence — a coffee, a miradouro, an hour watching the river go by. Build one day with nothing in it and do not feel obliged to fill it.

02 Three things that don't move

Read these
before you book

Two are about getting in, and they are new since you left Toronto. The third is about a Monday, and it costs you a day if you don't see it coming.

ETIAS may be live by the time you fly

Buy it anyway — €20

ETIAS, the EU's travel authorisation for visa-exempt nationals, is scheduled to launch in the last quarter of 2026. It then runs a six-month transitional period in which travellers without one may still be admitted provided they meet all other entry conditions, followed by a six-month grace period after that.

On a Q4 2026 start, 14 February 2027 sits inside the transitional window and you are legally fine without one. But the start date has slipped repeatedly and has not been confirmed by date, only by quarter — and airlines have historically enforced new authorisations at the gate more bluntly than borders do.

What to doApply the moment the portal opens, from europa.eu/etias and nowhere else. It is €20, valid three years or until your passport expires, and most applications are approved in minutes. Any site taking ETIAS applications before the official launch is fraudulent. Twenty euros against being denied boarding in Casablanca on the last leg of a four-month trip is not a close call.

Lisbon airport, EES, and your first Schengen entry

Budget 90 minutes

The EU's Entry/Exit System became fully operational on 10 April 2026 and replaced passport stamping outright. As a Canadian arriving from a non-Schengen country you get the full first-time enrolment: facial image plus four fingerprints, taken at the border, with your record created from scratch.

You will do this at the worst possible airport for it. Humberto Delgado topped Europe's 2026 airport stress ranking with a perfect score of 100/100 — 49.95% on-time departures, the worst of the thirty major airports studied, roughly 20-minute security waits, and 36.1 million passengers through a terminal built for far fewer. Portugal deployed 170 extra border officers in July 2026 and the Prime Minister said in August that conditions had "improved substantially"; treat that as a direction of travel, not a guarantee.

The leverPortugal was the second country to adopt the EES pre-enrolment app — do the data capture on your phone before you fly and you shorten the booth time considerably. Then don't book anything before 19:00 on arrival day. On the way home, be at the airport three hours before the Toronto flight, not two.

Monday 15 February is a closed day

Reorder the week

Your first full day is a Monday, and Portugal's national monuments and state museums close on Mondays. Jerónimos Monastery, the Belém Tower, the Coach Museum, the Ancient Art museum and the Gulbenkian are all shut. Belém — the single densest half-day in Lisbon — is simply unavailable.

What stays open: the whole of Sintra (Parques de Sintra monuments and Quinta da Regaleira run daily), São Jorge Castle, the trams and funiculars, Time Out Market, LX Factory, and every viewpoint, alley and pastelaria in the city.

The fixMonday is the walking day — Alfama, Graça, Mouraria, the miradouros, Bairro Alto — and Belém moves to Wednesday. Don't put Sintra on Monday either: it's open, but a Monday Sintra wastes the one day Lisbon's indoor half is shut.

03 The open question

Porto:
in or out

Seven nights is the longest single stop since Japan, and it's enough that "Lisbon and Sintra" starts to feel like it leaves room. Porto is about three hours north on the Alfa Pendular and it is a genuinely different city. The question is whether, on day 118 of a 130-day trip, you want another set of bags and another check-in.

Recommended

One base, seven nights

Lisbon ×7→ Sintra day→ Cascais day→ LIS

You unpack once, for the first time since Tokyo. Every day trip runs out and back on a commuter train, so the bag never moves and there is no check-out morning eating a day. Sintra on Tuesday, Belém on Wednesday, the coast on Friday, and two days with nothing scheduled — which, at this point in the trip, is the feature.

The honest case: Lisbon rewards depth. The city is a set of hills with different characters and you only start to feel the difference around day four. A week here is not padding.

Why the recommendation stays put

Every other stop on this trip has been a compromise against a clock. This one isn't, and the instinct to spend the surplus on another city is the same instinct that produced twenty-six stops in four months. The marginal value of Porto is high on a two-week Portugal trip and low as the 28th city in a row. You will not remember it well. You will remember the week you stopped moving.

If Porto is a real want rather than a reflex, the better version is to cut it loose entirely and come back for the Douro in autumn, when the valley is worth the journey and the weather cooperates.

What's cut either way

The Algarve. Three hours south for beaches at 15°C in a sea at 15°C. February is the wrong month and you have been warm since October.

Évora and the Alentejo. Roman temple, bone chapel, cork oaks, very good food, and best with a car — which is one more thing to rent, park and return on the last week of a long trip.

Madeira and the Azores. Separate flights, separate trips. Madeira in particular deserves a week of its own.

06 The month

Wet, mild,
and empty

February is the last of Lisbon's wet season and the quietest month of its year. Expect 16°C by day and 9°C at night, about 85mm of rain across fourteen rainy days, five hours of sunshine a day and eleven hours of daylight. That sounds worse than it is: Atlantic rain here arrives in fronts, not drizzle — a hard hour, then blue sky and wet limestone, which is when the city looks best.

What to pack out of the SaharaYou will arrive from a desert with nothing waterproof. A light rain shell and shoes with grip are the two things worth buying in Marrakech or at Lisbon's first Decathlon. Lisbon's pavement is calçada portuguesa — polished limestone cobbles, laid on hills, lethal when wet. This is the single most common way visitors hurt themselves here.

Carnival is five days earlyShrove Tuesday 2027 falls on 9 February, which you spend in Morocco. It isn't a statutory holiday in Portugal anyway — an optional one that some businesses take — but the Loulé and Torres Vedras parades, the interesting ones, will be over before you land. You arrive into Lent, which mostly means the city is quiet.

The upside of the monthNo queue at Jerónimos. A table at any restaurant you want. Room rates at their annual floor, on the one stop where you're paying for seven nights. Sintra without July's crush — still busy, but navigable. And sunset at around 18:15, so the evenings are long enough to be worth something.

07 The day trip

Sintra,
done properly

Sintra is a mountain covered in nineteenth-century follies, forty minutes from Rossio station, and it defeats more day-trippers than anywhere else in Portugal. The failure mode is always the same: arrive at 11:00, queue for the bus, queue for Pena, run out of daylight, see two things badly. Catch a train before nine and the whole day changes.

The order of operations

07:50 Rossio. €2.55 single, or €2.05 on zapping with a Navegante card (€0.50, bought once at any metro machine). Forty minutes. Rossio queues badly between 10:00 and noon — before nine there is no queue at all. Oriente also works and is seven minutes longer.

09:00 straight up to Pena. Bus 434, the Circuito da Pena, runs a one-way 10km loop — station, Moorish Castle, Pena, Sintra Vila, station. Low season it runs 09:30–18:20 with four departures an hour between 10:00 and 17:00. The 24-hour ticket is €13.50 and covers all Sintra buses including the 435 to Monserrate; a €4.55 single exists on paper and is routinely withdrawn from sale at busy times.

Book Pena and Regaleira before you leave Lisbon. Pena's Essential Visit is €20 and the Full Tour €45; park-only is €12 and, on a clear day, is a defensible choice — the exterior is the point and the interiors are crowded. Quinta da Regaleira now runs timed entry at €20, with split hours of 10:00–12:00 and 13:00–17:30 and a hard midday clear-out; its own site has been known to sell out roughly 48 hours ahead.

Do not drive. The historic centre is permanently closed to traffic, the upper Estrada da Pena closes to cars at busy times, and parking fills before 09:00. A car turns Sintra into a day of gridlock.

What's under scaffolding

Parques de Sintra publishes an "Open for Works" page and several things on it run into your window. Monserrate's roof restoration runs through March 2027; the Moorish Castle visitor centre is being rebuilt until January 2027; and the Royal Chapel at the Sintra National Palace is closed for full restoration until February 2028. Pena's park is still repairing damage from Storm Martinho in March 2025, with the Fern Garden and greenhouses affected indefinitely.

None of this closes a monument outright, but it changes what you get for €12. Check the works page the week you book — it is updated and specific.

The three-monument day, and why two is better

The standard Sintra circuit is Pena, the Moorish Castle and Quinta da Regaleira. It is achievable before dark in February only if you are on the 07:50 train and ruthless about lunch. Two done well beats three done at a jog — and if you cut one, cut the Moorish Castle: it is a ruined wall on a ridge with a superb view, and the view is free from the Pena park anyway.

The connoisseur's swap is Monserrate for the Moorish Castle — a Moorish-revival fantasy in a subtropical garden, ten minutes out on the 435, and reliably the emptiest of the major houses. Roof works notwithstanding, its interior plasterwork is the finest thing in Sintra.

What the card doesn't cover

Pena Palace is not included — the card gets you roughly 10% off, €20 down to €18. Quinta da Regaleira is independent and not included at all. The Sintra 434 bus is Scotturb, not Carris. So the two most expensive tickets of your week and the transport that reaches them all sit outside the card.

The other catch is the clock. Seven nights against a 72-hour maximum means the card only pays if you stack Belém, the castle and the museums into three consecutive days — which collides with the Monday closures and with Sintra wanting Tuesday. Run the numbers above honestly before buying; for a slow week, pay-as-you-go plus a €7.25 day pass on the heavy days usually wins.

Everyday transport, without the card

Buy a Navegante occasional card for €0.50 at any metro machine and load it with zapping credit. That gives you about €1.66 a ride on metro, bus, tram and the funiculars, against €1.80–€2.20 for a paper single, and it also pays for the Sintra and Cascais trains. A 24-hour unlimited pass is €7.25, or about €10.35 with the ferries.

The lifts are priced separately and steeply: Santa Justa €5.15 return, Glória, Bica and Lavra €3.70 each. The Glória is worth it once. Santa Justa is not — the same view is free from the Carmo ruins terrace at the top, reached on foot in four minutes.

EatingLunch is the bargain — a prato do dia with soup, wine and coffee runs €10–15 in any tasca that hasn't been discovered. Order the fish of the day whole and grilled. Bread, olives and cheese put on your table at the start are the couvert and you are charged for what you touch; wave it away without embarrassment if you don't want it.

CoffeeA bica is an espresso and costs around a euro standing at the counter, more at a table, most on a terrace. Three prices for one coffee is the national system, not a scam. Galão is the milky one, in a glass.

FadoWorth one night, and the difference between a good and bad fado house is total. Avoid anywhere with a photograph of a fado singer outside. Alfama and Mouraria have the real ones; they take reservations, they expect you to be silent while someone sings, and the minimum spend is the price of admission.

The pastelPastéis de Belém, in Belém, is the original 1837 recipe and worth the queue once — the takeaway line moves far faster than it looks. Manteigaria, with branches across the city, is the better everyday one. Cinnamon goes on at the table, not in the kitchen.
