01 The time audit

Five days
is three days

Siem Reap to Kuala Lumpur is a short hop — around two hours, and only one hour forward on the clock, so nothing here costs you sleep. What it does cost is both ends of the window. The 16th is a flying day and the 20th is a flying day, and one of the three days in between contains a four-hour move between your two bases. Every plan below is built against three usable days rather than five.

No jet lag, no dawn giftCambodia is UTC+7, Malaysia UTC+8. You arrive fully functional, which is good — but it also means Japan's trick of waking at five and owning the city doesn't repeat here. Worse, Malaysia keeps the whole country on UTC+8 despite sitting near 101°E, so the clock runs about half an hour ahead of the sun. Mid-December in KL: sunrise around 07:10, sunset around 19:05. "Early" on this leg means 07:30.

KUL is the exit, not just the entranceYour onward flight to Colombo leaves Kuala Lumpur. That single fact governs the whole routing question below — if you end the stop in Penang, the last morning contains a domestic flight feeding an international one, on the same day, with no slack.

The 19th is half a travel dayKL and Georgetown are 365 km apart. Whichever direction you do it in and whichever way you travel, the move eats four to five hours door to door. Section 07 does that arithmetic properly.

02 Two things that don't move

The coast is right,
the timing isn't

One of these is the reason Malaysia sits in December at all. The other is the price you pay for it, and it's the one worth acting on this week rather than next.

You're on the correct coast

The routing works

The northeast monsoon runs roughly November to March and it lands on the east side of the peninsula. Kota Bharu takes 600 mm-plus in each of November and December; Terengganu and Kelantan flood most years. KL and Penang, sheltered behind the Titiwangsa range, sit outside all of it.

Penang specifically has just come off its own wet peak — its heaviest months are September through November, and rainfall drops by roughly half into December. You are arriving on the falling edge.

The honest caveatKuala Lumpur is not dry in December — it takes something like 230 mm, most of it in late-afternoon thunderstorms that arrive fast, dump hard and clear inside an hour. That is a scheduling fact, not a weather problem: put the outdoor things before 15:00 and the museums, malls and hawker halls after.

You've landed in school holidays

Book now, not later

Malaysia's end-of-year school break runs through December, and Singapore's overlaps it. Georgetown fills with domestic and Singaporean families from mid-December onward, and hotel rates move with them. Your window sits squarely inside it.

The sharper problem is the train. ETS seats between KL and Butterworth sell out during school holidays — KTMB routinely adds extra December services precisely because the scheduled ones go. Turning up at KL Sentral hoping for a seat is not a plan for 19 December.

What to doBook the ETS the moment the window opens — KTMB releases seats somewhere between 30 days and three months out, and the release date itself moves. Set a reminder for early October and check weekly. Same for Georgetown accommodation.

03 The open question

Which end
gets Penang

Both cities are already on the list, so the question isn't whether — it's the order. And the order is not cosmetic here, because the last day of this stop is an international departure from KUL. One shape protects that flight; the other buys you an extra half-day of Georgetown and puts the flight at risk.

Recommended

Penang first

KUL→ Penang ×3→ KL ×1→ KUL

Land at KUL around midday on the 16th and connect straight onto a domestic hop north — PEN is 50 minutes away and served constantly by AirAsia, Batik and Firefly. You're eating in Georgetown that evening. Two clear days on the island, then the ETS south on the morning of the 19th.

The departure is safe. You sleep in Kuala Lumpur on the 19th, thirty minutes from KL Sentral and a 28-minute train from the terminal. Nothing about the Colombo flight depends on a domestic connection running to time.

The cost is that Kuala Lumpur gets one evening and one morning. That's Merdeka 118 at sunset, dinner on Jalan Alor, and Batu Caves before the airport — which is, honestly, most of what a first pass at KL should be.

If KL deserves a real day

KL first

KUL→ KL ×2→ Penang ×2→ PEN→KUL

Settle into KL on arrival, spend the 17th on it properly — Batu Caves at opening, the Islamic Arts Museum, Chinatown, Merdeka 118 at golden hour — then take the ETS north on the morning of the 18th with an optional Ipoh stopover built in.

The catch is the 20th. You have to fly PEN→KUL and connect to Colombo the same morning. Book it as a single through ticket so a delay is the airline's problem, or leave a minimum four-hour gap on separate tickets and accept that a cancelled morning hop costs you the leg.

Worth it if a full day in Kuala Lumpur matters more to you than a slow second morning of Penang hawker breakfasts. Reasonable people differ.

The third option, briefly: don't go to Penang

Four nights in Kuala Lumpur alone would be a defensible trip — Batu Caves, the Islamic Arts Museum, Kampung Baru, a Melaka day trip, and enough evenings to eat properly. But Georgetown is the better city for what this leg is for. It's a walkable UNESCO core of shophouses and clan jetties, it has the best street food in Southeast Asia by most reckonings, and unlike KL it cannot be replicated by any of the other twenty-six stops. KL is a very good regional capital; Penang is specific.

The one genuine argument for staying put: you have just come off Siem Reap and Angkor, and you fly to Colombo on the 20th. If what you need in the middle of that is four nights in one bed and a city that runs on air conditioning, take it and don't apologise.

What's cut either way

Melaka. The other half of the Straits-Chinese story, two hours south of KL by bus. It needs an overnight to be worth anything and a day trip is four hours of coach for a very touristed riverfront. Georgetown does Peranakan heritage better and you're already going there.

Cameron Highlands. Tea plantations, cool air, mossy forest — and a punishing switchback road from Ipoh that eats most of a day each way. A two-night proposition minimum.

Taman Negara and Borneo. The rainforest and the orangutans are the reason a lot of people come to Malaysia, and both are separate trips. Borneo is a two-hour flight and another week. Note it for later.

Langkawi. An hour's flight from Penang and a good beach, but the northwest in December is on the wrong side of decent — and you have the Maldives on the 24th. Skip.

06 The point of Penang

Eat on
the clock

Georgetown's food is not a restaurant scene, it's a shift system. Individual dishes belong to specific hours, and the good stalls sell out and shut rather than stay open. Turn up for Hokkien mee at two in the afternoon and you will be told, politely, that it's finished — because it is. This chart is the single most useful thing on this page.

The five dishes that are actually Penang's

Hokkien mee, called prawn mee here — an orange broth cooked for hours from prawn shells and pork bones, noodles, egg, sambal on the side. A morning dish. The broth takes all night to make and the best stalls are done by noon.

Assam laksa — tamarind and poached mackerel, torch ginger flower, mint, pineapple, a spoon of shrimp paste stirred in at the table. Sour, funky, nothing else tastes like it. Mid-day into early evening.

Char kway teow — flat rice noodles, cockles, Chinese sausage, egg, dark soy, cooked one plate at a time over a fire hot enough to be frightening. Look for a charcoal wok; that's where the smoke flavour comes from. Mostly an evening dish, though a few morning stalls exist.

Nasi kandar — the Indian-Muslim rice-and-curry counter. Point at things; ask for banjir and they flood the plate with several gravies at once. Hameediyah on Campbell Street has been doing it since 1907. Open more or less all day.

Cendol — shaved ice, green pandan noodles, coconut milk, palm sugar. Penang Road Famous Teochew Cendol, in the alley off Lebuh Keng Kwee, has been there since the 1930s and there is always a queue. It moves fast.

Mechanics

Cash and QR. Hawker stalls are cash-first; many now also take DuitNow QR, which you can't easily use on a foreign card. Carry small notes — RM50 in fives and tens covers a day of eating comfortably.

How it works in a coffee shop. You sit down, someone comes to take a drink order — that's the person who owns the shop. The food stalls around the edge are separate businesses; you order from each one at the stall and they bring it to your table. Pay each seller as they deliver.

Where to go. Chulia Street after dark for the street carts, Kimberley Street for duck kway chap and koay teow th'ng, Lebuh Presgrave and New Lane for evening hawker centres, Gurney Drive for the big touristed night market — good, not the best, and worth one visit for the scale of it.

Closing days. Nearly every stall takes one day off a week and it's rarely a weekend. Two minutes of checking before you walk twenty minutes for a specific plate of noodles saves the walk.

The three things the calculator can't show you

Firefly flies to Subang, not KLIA. SZB is the old city airport, half an hour from central KL instead of the full KLIA Ekspres run. If you're flying this leg, a Firefly ticket into Subang quietly deletes about forty minutes and RM40 of the flight's overhead. It's the single best fare-class decision on this stop.

The Butterworth end is a walk. The ETS terminates at Penang Sentral in Butterworth, on the mainland. The ferry to Georgetown is RM2 and about twenty minutes across, but the walk from the platform to the ferry gates through the terminal building is longer and more uphill than it looks — allow twenty minutes for it, more with luggage. First ferry 06:30, last one back from Georgetown 23:30.

The train is the nicer four hours. ETS runs at 140 km/h through palm plantations and limestone karst, with power sockets, a trolley and real legroom in Platinum. It also drops you in the middle of Kuala Lumpur rather than on the edge of Sepang. On a trip this long, a leg where you get to sit still and look out of a window has value the arithmetic doesn't capture.

The stopover: Ipoh

Every ETS between KL and Butterworth stops at Ipoh, roughly the halfway mark and about two and a half hours from KL Sentral. It is Malaysia's quiet third food city — white coffee, bean sprout chicken, and a compact colonial old town of shophouses around a genuinely beautiful 1917 railway station.

Break the journey with a locker and three hours and you can walk Concubine Lane, eat at a kopitiam that's been open since the 1930s, and be back on a later train. Booking it means two separate ETS tickets, KL–Ipoh and Ipoh–Butterworth, which is fine but does double your exposure to sold-out December services. Only worth it in the KL-first shape, where the move happens on the 18th and the day has room in it.

Weather31–33°C by day, 24–26°C at night, humidity high and constant. Rain arrives as short violent afternoon storms rather than all-day drizzle. Carry a small umbrella rather than a rain jacket — a shell in this humidity is unwearable.

LanguageBahasa Malaysia is the national language and English is genuinely widespread, more so than anywhere else on this leg of the trip. In Georgetown you'll hear Hokkien, Mandarin, Tamil and Malay in the same block. You will not need a phrase book.
