01 The time audit

Four days
is two days

The itinerary says 1–4 November, and unlike every stop before it, this one costs you almost no jet lag — Shanghai and Taipei share a time zone, and the hop is under two hours. What it doesn't give you is time: land early afternoon on the 1st, fly onward to Bangkok on the morning of the 4th, and what's left is two full days sandwiched between two fragments. That's a tight budget for a route that wants both Taroko Gorge and a night market in every plan.

No jet lag, for onceChina Eastern or EVA fly Shanghai–Taipei in under two hours with zero time-zone change — the first stop on this trip where your body clock doesn't need resetting. Land around 13:00, clear immigration, and you have a genuine afternoon rather than a write-off evening.

Taoyuan, not SongshanMost Shanghai flights land at Taoyuan (TPE), 40km west of the city — the Airport MRT gets you to Taipei Main Station in about 35–50 minutes depending on express or local. If your flight happens to route through Songshan (TSA) instead, you're already inside the city; either way, buy an EasyCard in the arrivals hall before you do anything else.

The 4th is a half-day, not a dayBangkok is a four-hour flight and Thailand is an hour behind Taiwan — budget the whole morning for the airport run and treat 3 November night as your last full evening in Taiwan.

02 Two things that don't move

Whichever way
you slice it

These two hold regardless of how you split Taipei and Taroko below — worth knowing before you get into the routing decision.

Taroko in 2026 is a smaller gorge

Nothing you can do

The 7.4-magnitude earthquake of April 2024 brought down cliff faces throughout the gorge, and recovery is measured in years, not months. Shakadang Trail, the Zhuilu Old Road and Baiyang Trail remain closed as of early 2026, and the main road through the gorge (Highway 8) is open to traffic only in short scheduled windows rather than freely, with landslide risk closing it outright after rain.

What's open: the entrance arch and visitor centre, the short Dekalun Trail, Tianxiang with its Xiangde Temple path, and Qixingtan Beach and the Chongde viewpoint on the coast, unaffected by the quake. Eternal Spring Shrine and the Buluowan suspension bridge are roadside-view only for now.

The reframeThis still isn't a bad two days — you're seeing marble cliffs and a river gorge few Western tourists get to before 2024's damage is fully repaired — it's just a different, smaller version of the trip than the one in every five-year-old blog post. Plan around the road's opening windows (Fig. 2 below), not around the old trail map.

The scooter needs paperwork you get at home

Handle before YYZ

Taiwan will not rent a scooter to a foreign licence alone. You need an International Driving Permit with a motorcycle ("A") endorsement — most Canadian provinces issue this through CAA, and it has to show you're licensed to ride a motorcycle at home, not just drive a car. Get it before you leave; there's no fixing this in Hualien.

Even with the endorsement, a growing number of shops near Taroko are tightening up and asking for it explicitly since the road restrictions began. A few still rent without it for a larger deposit, but don't plan the day around finding one of those.

The leverIf the IDP endorsement genuinely isn't obtainable before you fly, book a guided minibus or taxi day tour into the gorge instead — several outfits run exactly this route from Hualien station and know the road's open windows better than you will.

03 The open question

Taroko:
day trip or overnight

Taipei and the flight to Bangkok are fixed points. Taroko sits roughly two hours each way by train, and the gorge road itself only opens in short scheduled windows — so the question isn't really "how many days," it's whether you sleep in Hualien or commute. Both shapes below fit the same 1–4 Nov window and both use the scooter.

Recommended

Hualien overnight

Taipei ×1→ Tianxiang/Hualien ×1→ Taipei ×1→ TPE

Train out early on day two, scooter into the gorge for the midday window, sleep in Tianxiang or Hualien, then a second crack at the road on an early window next morning before heading back. Two windows instead of one, and no four-hour same-day round trip eating into gorge time.

The routing trick: the Hualien line runs through Ruifang on the way back — get off there instead of riding straight through, and Jiufen is a fifteen-minute bus up the hill. You reach Taipei's fixed point and Jiufen's open question in the same afternoon.

Why the recommendation is the overnight, not the day trip

With only two full days total, the day-trip shape spends one of them almost entirely on a train and a scooter, and still only banks one road-opening window if the return train has any padding built in — miss the window and you're waiting two hours at a checkpoint with the gorge still ahead of you. The overnight costs you nothing extra in nights (you have three either way) and buys a second window, a sunrise in the mountains, and the Jiufen stopover on the way back essentially for free.

If you'd rather not deal with hotel logistics for a single night, the day-trip shape is genuinely fine — just book the earliest outbound train you can and treat any road window past the first as a bonus, not the plan.

What's cut either way

Sun Moon Lake. Central Taiwan's other big scenic draw, and a beautiful place — but it's a three-hour bus from Taichung in the opposite direction from Hualien. Not on a four-day trip with Taroko already booked.

Kenting. The beach town at Taiwan's southern tip is excellent in November's dry season, but it's a full day's travel from Taipei each way. Save it for a trip built around the south.

Alishan. The other great mountain railway and sunrise-above-the-clouds spot, reached from Chiayi in the centre-south. Wrong direction again — this itinerary only has room for one mountain, and the route already picked Taroko.

06 The gorge question

Smaller, and
still worth it

You asked for Taroko by scooter, so this is the honest version. The road through the gorge is a single lane blasted into a marble cliff face, and since the 2024 quake it's managed almost like a level crossing — open to traffic in scheduled windows, closed the rest of the time, and shut outright after rain while crews check for loose rock.

What's actually open, area by area

Near the entrance — the arch, the visitor centre, and the short, steep Dekalun Trail (about ninety minutes return) are all open without restriction. This is where a rushed day trip ends up spending most of its time if the road windows don't cooperate.

Tianxiang, deeper in — reachable inside a road window, with the Xiangde Temple trail and a suspension bridge still open. This is also where the overnight shape sleeps, which is what buys the second window the next morning.

Closed for now — Shakadang Trail, the Zhuilu Old Road (which needed a permit even before 2024), and Baiyang Trail's tunnel-and-waterfall walk are all shut, some indefinitely. Eternal Spring Shrine and the Buluowan suspension bridge are visible from the road but not walkable.

The honest comparison: this is not the Taroko of ten years of guidebooks. It is still a marble gorge with a river running through it and cliffs on both sides, seen from a scooter seat inside a real, functioning national park recovering in real time. Go for what's there rather than what's closed.

Getting the scooter, and the train that gets you there

Rental shops cluster around Hualien Station and, per the IDP note above, increasingly require the motorcycle endorsement. Expect to leave a passport copy and a deposit; a day rate for a 125cc scooter runs roughly NT$500–700.

The Taroko and Puyuma Express trains cover Taipei–Hualien in about two hours for around NT$440 one-way; slower trains take three to four. Book ahead — these specific express services sell out on weekends and holidays, and 1 November lands on a Sunday. Reserve online at the TRA site (railway.gov.tw) or through 7-Eleven's ibon kiosk once in Taiwan, and pick tickets up at any 7-Eleven counter for a small fee.

WeatherExpect 24–26°C by day and 18–20°C at night in Taipei, cooling further in the mountains around Taroko. November is one of the driest months on the calendar and typhoon season is all but over, though a late storm remains a small possibility this far into autumn.

Getting into townThe Airport MRT from Taoyuan to Taipei Main Station runs express (35 min) and local (50 min) services on the same line — buy the EasyCard first, then just tap through either train.

Booking trainsReserve the Hualien legs specifically; everything else — MRT, city buses, the Jiufen bus — runs frequently enough to wing it with an EasyCard in hand.

EatingNight market food is the meal, not the snack before one — pepper buns and stinky tofu at Raohe, the sheer size of Shilin, or Ningxia's tighter, more local lineup. Budget NT$150–300 for a full night of grazing and reserve nothing.
