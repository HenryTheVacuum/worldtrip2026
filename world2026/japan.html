01 The time audit

Seven days
is five days

The itinerary says 15–22 October. Flying west over the Pacific you lose the 15th to the date line — Air Canada leaves Toronto early afternoon and lands at Haneda late on the 16th. Departure day is a travel day too. What you actually get is five full days and two fragments, and every plan below is built against that number rather than the calendar one.

The jet-lag giftToronto to Tokyo is thirteen hours forward. For the first four or five days you will be wide awake at 4am and useless by 9pm. That is the single best thing that will happen to you here — Japan's most crowded sights are empty and beautiful at 6:30. Build the whole week around dawn and go to bed early without guilt.

Haneda, not NaritaYou land at the good airport. Keikyū or the Monorail puts you in central Tokyo in about 30 minutes, against 60–90 from Narita. If the airline tries to rebook you onto Narita during a schedule change, push back — it costs you two hours of a week that has none spare.

Arrival evening is not a dayLand ~16:00, clear immigration, reach a hotel around 18:30 still wearing yesterday. Plan exactly one thing: dinner within walking distance. Anything more ambitious gets abandoned.

02 Two things that don't move

Whichever way
you slice it

These two hold regardless of whether Kyoto makes the cut — worth knowing before you get into the routing decision below.

Mid-October is not autumn

Nothing you can do

Japan's famous colour runs from the mountains downward over about eight weeks. Kyoto and Tokyo peak in late November. Hakone in early November. On 15–22 October, every garden on this route — Kyoto's included, if you add it — will be entirely green.

The only overlap is elevation. Nikko's Lake Chūzenji, at about 1,300 m, turns in mid-to-late October — but that's a Tokyo day trip you won't have room for once Hiroshima is in the plan.

The consolationYou dodge the crush. Late-November Kyoto is a scrum — booked out, queued, shoulder to shoulder at every gate. In mid-October you get the same gardens at maybe a third of the density. Green and empty beats red and immobile.

Fuji in October

Better than you'd think

You cannot climb it — the season is July to 10 September, and all four trails now carry a mandatory ¥4,000 fee and a reservation anyway. But climbing was never the plan, and October is a good month to see it.

Summer air is hazy and the mountain hides behind cloud most days. Visibility climbs steadily through autumn into a winter peak. Mid-October also usually catches the first snow cap, which is the version everyone pictures.

The leverTime of day, not day of week. Clear mornings collapse into cloud by lunchtime almost year-round. Be at the lake within two hours of sunrise, which your jet lag makes trivial.

Tokyo and Hiroshima are fixed points — six nights between them. Adding Kyoto doesn't cost you a spare day, because there isn't one; it takes a full day from Tokyo and turns two travel days into three. Both shapes below fit the same 15–22 Oct window.

Why the recommendation cuts Kyoto rather than Tokyo or Hiroshima

Hiroshima is the newer addition and the one with a hard floor — under two nights, either the Peace Park or Miyajima gets rushed, and rushing the Peace Park specifically is a bad trade to make for a stopover. Tokyo at one day is thin for a first visit to a city this dense. Kyoto is the only one of the three where a zero-night version already exists: the Okayama stopover above, or simply banking Kyoto for a future trip when it isn't fighting green gardens for its own colour season anyway.

If Kyoto is non-negotiable for you, the one-night version is genuinely workable — it's the same trade Tue 21 makes in the day-by-day below. Use the toggle in the next section to see exactly what changes.

What's cut either way

Kanazawa and Kenroku-en. One of the Three Great Gardens, and a beautiful city — but it sits north of the main line, a full extra day to reach. Okayama's Kōraku-en is free and on the way.

Osaka. Thirty minutes from Kyoto and a genuinely good eating city, but it doesn't do anything Tokyo hasn't already done for you at larger scale. On a 27-country trip, it's an easy skip.

Nara. Forty-five minutes from Kyoto; Tōdai-ji and Isuien garden are superb. Only fits if Kyoto gets two nights, which neither shape above gives it.

06 The garden question

Green, and
that's fine

You said gardens on the way to Hiroshima, so this is the honest version, whether or not Kyoto makes the final cut. Japan's colour arrives by altitude, sliding down the mountains through November. Your window sits three to six weeks early everywhere you'll be, with one exception at 1,300 metres.

The Three Great Gardens, and why only one is on your route

Kōraku-en, Okayama — fifteen minutes' walk from a Shinkansen station that sits directly between Kyoto and Hiroshima, next to the black castle. A two-hour stop with your bag in a coin locker. This is the one that's free.

Kenroku-en, Kanazawa — arguably the finest of the three, and a full day of your five to reach. Cut it.

Kairaku-en, Mito — north-east of Tokyo, wrong direction, and it's a plum garden that exists for February. Not this trip, not this month.

The uncomfortable truth is that the Three Great Gardens are a Meiji-era list, and Kyoto's temple gardens are better than any of them — smaller, stranger, more deliberate. Ryōan-ji's rock garden, Tōfuku-ji's modernist checkerboard moss, the Daitoku-ji subtemples, Murin-an's Meiji water garden with Higashiyama borrowed behind it. That's the case for adding Kyoto. If the answer is no, Okayama is the free consolation and Kōraku-en is a genuinely good garden in its own right — just not this good.

Two that need booking weeks ahead, and are worth it

Katsura Rikyū — the imperial villa in west Kyoto, widely held to be the greatest garden in Japan. Free, but visits are guided, capped, and booked through the Imperial Household Agency. Slots open roughly three months out and go.

Saihō-ji, the moss temple — 120-odd species of moss under maples, entry by advance reservation only, around ¥4,000, and you copy a sutra before they let you into the garden. That gatekeeping is why it's quiet.

Both are booked online. Do it the week you finalise the flights, not the week you fly.

07 The mountain

Odds on Fuji

Fuji is visible on well under half the days in a year and hardly at all in summer. October sits on the rising edge of the good season — roughly two mornings in five, improving through November into a winter peak near eighty percent. These are rules of thumb rather than measured statistics, but the shape is right and the shape is what matters.

HakoneLake Ashi at first light, from the Moto-Hakone end, with the shrine's torii standing in the water and the cone behind it. Sitting on the Tōkaidō line means you continue west afterwards instead of doubling back. Onsen and a kaiseki dinner are the bonus, and the ropeway over the Ōwakudani vents is genuinely strange. Check the volcanic-gas status the morning you go; the ropeway closes without much warning.

KawaguchikoThe Chūreitō pagoda shot — pagoda, cherry-less October slopes, Fuji beyond — is the postcard, and it is a real place with 400 steps. But Kawaguchiko is a dead end: you either return to Shinjuku or take the bus down to Mishima to rejoin the Shinkansen. It has also become extremely crowded. Choose it if the specific photograph matters more than the day it costs.

The free oneGoing Tokyo→Kyoto, sit on the right — seats D and E. Fuji appears about forty minutes out, near Shin-Fuji, for roughly ninety seconds. On a clear morning it is startling, and you paid nothing for it.

08 The rail maths

Skip the
rail pass

The nationwide JR Pass stopped being an automatic buy in October 2023 when the 7-day ordinary fare went from ¥29,650 to ¥50,000. It gets worse for you specifically: a further rise to ¥53,000 lands on 1 October 2026 through overseas sales channels, two weeks before you arrive. What the pass wouldn't cover anyway

The Odakyū Romancecar into Hakone, the whole Hakone loop, Kyoto's buses and subway, and the Hiroshima airport bus are all private or non-JR. A meaningful share of your moving is outside the pass entirely, which makes the arithmetic worse than the table above suggests.

Regional passes are a different question and occasionally do win — JR West's Sanyō–San'in and Kansai–Hiroshima passes can cover a Kyoto→Hiroshima run if you add Kyoto. Do the sum against your actual legs rather than the brochure; on a six-night trip the margins are thin either way. Prices move often — check before buying.

WeatherExpect 21–23°C by day and 13–15°C at night across Tokyo, Kyoto and Hiroshima, with humidity finally gone. Late typhoons are possible into October but rare by mid-month. Layers, a light shell, and shoes you can put twenty kilometres a day through.

GionKyoto closed several private alleys in the Gion district to visitors after years of bad behaviour, with fines attached. The public streets are still open and still beautiful; don't chase geiko down side streets, and don't photograph people without asking.

Booking trainsSmart EX or the ticket machines both work, and you can reserve Shinkansen seats a month out. Reserve the Tokyo→Kyoto leg specifically so you can pick a right-hand window for Fuji. Everything else can be bought the day before.

EatingThe best meals of the week will cost under ¥1,500 and be eaten standing up or at a counter with eight seats. Reserve one proper dinner — a kaiseki in Kyoto or a ryokan meal in Hakone — and improvise everything else.
