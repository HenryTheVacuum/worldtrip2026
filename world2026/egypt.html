01 The time audit

Eight days,
and it ends
on the 20th

The itinerary says 14–21 January. Riyadh to Cairo is a four-hour hop with about twenty departures a day, so the 14th is a genuine arrival evening rather than a lost day. The problem is the other end: the nonstop to Zanzibar doesn't fly on the 21st, and the one that gets you there on the right date leaves Cairo at 22:35 on Wednesday the 20th. Everything below is built against six full days, not eight.

No jet lag to spendRiyadh is UTC+3, Cairo UTC+2. You arrive one hour behind, which is nothing. Unlike Japan, Egypt will not hand you 6am for free — and Egypt is a country where the whole game is being at the gate when it opens. Set alarms. Every site on this route is transformed by arriving first, and by 10:30 the coach groups have landed on all of them.

The night train is a free nightCairo to Aswan overnight on the Abela/Watania sleeper leaves you a hotel night and a travel day ahead. It is not a luxury train and the food is airline food, but it converts dead time into distance. Booking opens four to five months out.

Late January is peakThis is the best weather Egypt has, which means it is also the most expensive and most crowded week of the year outside Christmas. Cruise cabins, the good Luxor hotels and Grand Egyptian Museum slots all want booking now, not in December.

02 Two things that don't move

One breaks
the itinerary

The first of these changes the shape of the stop and needs deciding before anything else is booked. The second is the reason this window was chosen in the first place, and it holds up.

There is no Thursday flight to Zanzibar

Breaks the plan as written — fix below

The handoff to Tanzania is Thursday 21 January. The only nonstop Cairo–Zanzibar service is EgyptAir's, launched 5 September 2026 as a twice-weekly Friday and Sunday operation. AeroRoutes shows a third weekly frequency added from 16 December 2026 — a Wednesday — and the aggregators now list the route as Wednesday, Friday and Sunday.

Published winter timings: MS819 departs Cairo 22:35, lands Zanzibar 05:15 the next morning on a 737-800. Northbound MS820 leaves Zanzibar 06:15. Cairo is UTC+2 and Zanzibar UTC+3, so that's about 5h40 in the air. On Thursday the 21st, nothing.

The fixTake Wednesday night's MS819 — depart Cairo 22:35 on the 20th, land Zanzibar 05:15 on the 21st. The Tanzania arrival date is unchanged and you gain a Zanzibar morning; Egypt loses only the evening of the 20th. This means ending in Luxor, not Aswan — Luxor has 4–6 daily flights to Cairo against Aswan's 2–3. If you'd rather keep Thursday as the travel day, Ethiopian via Addis is about 7h40 and Kenya Airways via Nairobi about 9h30.

January is the right month, emphatically

The window is the best part of the plan

Luxor averages a 23°C daytime high in January against 41°C in July; Aswan 24°C against 42°C. Cairo sits around 19°C. This is the difference between walking the Valley of the Kings and enduring it — the valley is a stone bowl with no shade and it bakes.

Nights are cold in the south — Luxor drops to about 6°C before dawn, and the Abu Simbel road at 04:30 is genuinely chilly. Pack one warm layer you won't otherwise use on this trip. Rain is effectively zero everywhere on this route.

Bonus timingRamadan 2027 is expected to begin around 8 February, more than two weeks after you leave, so opening hours are normal throughout. Coptic Christmas falls on 7 January, a week before you land. Nothing in the window closes.

What still needs confirming

No source publishes a full day-of-week grid for January 2027. The Wednesday/Friday/Sunday pattern is inferred from EgyptAir's announced third weekly frequency starting 16 December 2026 — which is a Wednesday — plus an aggregator listing. Pull the actual operated history for MS819 for a few weeks either side before you book, rather than trusting a schedule-site day label; that has been wrong more than once on this trip.

Also worth asking EgyptAir: whether Luxor → Cairo → Zanzibar sells as a single ticket. If it does, a missed domestic connection becomes their problem rather than yours. If it doesn't, leave at least four hours at Cairo, re-check your bag, and confirm whether the domestic and international terminals need a landside transfer.

04 The open question

Cruise,
or don't

Six days between Cairo and a Wednesday-night flight. The classic Egypt trip puts a Nile cruise in the middle; the problem is that cruises sail on fixed weekly departure days — Luxor on Monday or Saturday, Aswan on Wednesday or Friday — and only one of those slots fits at all. Both shapes below end at Cairo in time for MS819.

Recommended

Fly and base

Cairo ×2→ night train→ Aswan ×2→ Luxor ×2→ CAI

South first, north second. Two Cairo days for Giza and the Grand Egyptian Museum, an overnight train that buys back a day, two Aswan days with Abu Simbel at opening, then the road north through Kom Ombo and Edfu into Luxor for the west and east banks.

Why it ends in Luxor: the exit flight is unforgiving and Luxor has 4–6 daily flights to Cairo against Aswan's 2–3. Ending north also means the last morning is Karnak at opening rather than a dash to an airport.

Why the recommendation drops the boat

A Nile cruise is a lovely thing and this is the wrong week for it. The sailing calendar and the flight calendar disagree by about a day, and the only overlap leaves you making a same-day domestic connection into an international departure that operates three times a week. That is the kind of arrangement that ruins the next stop rather than this one.

The trade is also lopsided on content. What the cruise uniquely gives you is Edfu and Kom Ombo — and both sit directly on the Aswan–Luxor road, where they are a three-hour stop rather than four nights. What it takes is Abu Simbel, which is not substitutable by anything.

What's cut in both shapes

Alexandria. Three hours each way from Cairo for a Graeco-Roman city that mostly isn't there any more. The library is a modern building and the catacombs are a half-hour. Not against Abu Simbel.

The Red Sea and Sinai. Dahab and the Blue Hole are superb and in completely the wrong direction. Note also that Canada advises against all travel to North Sinai and non-essential travel to the northern part of South Sinai.

The White Desert and Siwa. Canada's advisory covers the Western Desert west of the Giza–Luxor–Aswan–Abu Simbel road. Your entire route sits inside the recommended corridor; keep it that way.

Dendera and Abydos. The best-preserved ceiling in Egypt and one of the great temples, both a long day trip north from Luxor. They need a seventh day you no longer have.

07 Why this window

Twenty-three,
not forty-one

Egypt's calendar is written by the sun, and Upper Egypt is the part that decides it. Luxor and Aswan are Saharan — bone dry, no moderating water, and in summer they run above 40°C by lunchtime. January is the floor, and the floor is the only time the Valley of the Kings, Karnak and Abu Simbel are pleasant rather than survivable.

The cold endNights in Upper Egypt are genuinely cold — Luxor drops to about 6°C before dawn, Aswan to 9°C. The dawn balloon flight, the 04:30 Abu Simbel road and a felucca after sunset all need a layer you will otherwise carry unused from Tokyo to Lisbon. Bring one.

The windJanuary is also when the Nile Valley gets a steady northerly. It makes feluccas sail beautifully and balloons cancel occasionally. Book the balloon for your first Luxor morning so a scrub has somewhere to go.

What January costs youPeak season pricing and peak season crowds. Every itinerary in Egypt runs the same clockwise loop at the same hours, so the difference between a 06:00 start and an 09:00 start is roughly the difference between forty people and four hundred.

08 The ticket maths

Skip the
passes

Egypt sells a Cairo Pass and a Luxor Pass, both around US$130, and both are sold as the obvious purchase. On a six-day trip they generally aren't. Tick the sites you'll actually enter and watch the point-to-point total fall short of the pass price. Note the standing irony: almost every site is now card-only, while the passes can only be bought with physical US dollars or euros.

What the passes don't cover

The Cairo Pass (US$130, five consecutive days) covers the Giza plateau including the Great Pyramid interior, Saqqara, Dahshur, the Citadel and the old Egyptian Museum at Tahrir. It does not cover the Grand Egyptian Museum or the National Museum of Egyptian Civilization — which, post-2025, are the two you most want. That exclusion is most of the argument against it.

The Luxor Pass comes in two grades: US$130 standard, or US$250 premium adding the tombs of Seti I and Nefertari. Buying a Cairo Pass first halves the Luxor price, which is the one arrangement where the maths can tip — a Cairo Pass plus a discounted premium Luxor Pass is $255 for a great deal of access, if you intend to enter genuinely everything.

Both need a passport photocopy and two passport photos, and are bought in person — at the Giza ticket office, the Egyptian Museum or the Citadel for Cairo. Cash only, USD or EUR. Do not arrive at Giza planning to buy one with a card.

GuidesEgypt is one of the few places on this trip where a licensed guide materially changes what you see — the reliefs are text, and unread they're decoration. Hire per-site or per-day rather than buying a packaged tour: roughly US$40–60 a day for a good English-speaking Egyptologist, arranged through your hotel.

ToutsGiza and Luxor west bank have the most persistent hustle you'll meet between Tokyo and Marrakech — camel rides, "the site is closed", unsolicited photos then a fee. A flat, friendly "la, shukran" and continuing to walk works. Never hand over your ticket or passport to anyone who isn't at a window.

PhotographyOrdinary cameras and phones are fine almost everywhere; some tombs charge a separate photo ticket and a few — Tutankhamun's among them — forbid it outright. Tripods generally require a permit. The rule is posted at each gate and enforced unevenly.

StomachBottled water only, including for teeth, and be careful with salads and ice outside good restaurants. Six days is exactly long enough for a bad day to cost you Abu Simbel. Carry rehydration salts; they weigh nothing.
