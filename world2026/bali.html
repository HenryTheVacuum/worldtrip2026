01 The time audit

Six days
is four days

Singapore to Denpasar is under three hours with no clock change — Singapore, Bali and Manila all sit on UTC+8, so this is the one leg of the trip that costs you nothing in jet lag. What it costs you is the other end: every non-stop to Manila leaves DPS between 00:55 and 08:35. Your last night is a 03:00 alarm, which makes the 25th a travel day and not a day.

Arrival is half a day, not noneLand around midday, and the airport-to-Ubud road eats 90 minutes to two hours of it. You still get a late afternoon and a dinner, which is more than the Tokyo arrival gave you. Do the longest transfer of the leg on day one, while you still have patience for a Balinese road.

The 03:00 alarm is the plan, not a failure of itThere is no civilised departure to Manila. Accept it, sleep on the Bukit forty minutes from the airport, and pack on the afternoon of the 24th. The upside is real: at three in the morning Bali's roads are empty for the only time all week.

Four full days, one islandBali is 5,780 km² and Indonesia is 1.9 million. This leg is not Indonesia; it is one small, extremely visited island in it. That's a reasonable choice for four days — see §06 — but it's worth naming.

02 The compromise

You booked
the wet season

The rest of Westbound chases dry windows. This stop doesn't, and the brief already admits it. November is when Bali's monsoon opens: rainfall roughly doubles from October's ~166 mm to ~332 mm, and stays there through March. That is the honest headline. The rest of this section is what it actually means day to day, which is less bad than the number looks.

The rain is real and it is daily

Not fixable, only scheduled around

Expect rain most days of your six. Humidity sits near 78%, days run 28°C and nights 23°C, and the sea is bath-warm. The west-coast surf — Canggu, Uluwatu, Padang Padang — turns messy and onshore as the winds swing, so if you were picturing glassy Bukit barrels, that's a July picture.

What it costs youSunset is less reliable, the ocean is murkier for snorkelling, and any plan that depends on a clear volcano — Batur at dawn, Agung behind the Sidemen valley — becomes a coin flip.

But it rains on a schedule

This is the whole strategy

Wet-season rain in Bali is not British drizzle. Mornings are usually clear; cloud builds through late morning; the storm arrives mid-afternoon, hits hard for thirty to ninety minutes, and clears. All-day rain happens but is not the norm in November.

So the leg runs on the same rhythm as the Japan page, for a different reason: every outdoor thing happens before 11:00, and every afternoon has an indoor fallback already chosen.

What it buys youLow season. Thinner crowds at Tegallalang and Uluwatu, better room availability, materially cheaper accommodation than July–August, waterfalls at full flow, and the east coast — Amed, Tulamben, the Nusa islands — calm while the west is choppy.

03 The open question

Two bases
or three

Ubud, Canggu and Uluwatu is the brief, and the order is already right — Ubud is the furthest, so it goes first while you're fresh, and the Bukit is nearest the airport, so it goes last before a 03:00 run. The question is whether Canggu earns a night or an afternoon. On this island the answer is decided by the roads, not the distances.

Recommended

Two bases

Ubud ×2→ Canggu afternoon→ Uluwatu ×3→ DPS

Canggu becomes a long lunch and a beach afternoon on the transfer day rather than a base. You unpack twice instead of three times, and the Bukit gets three nights — enough for two proper dawns on the staircase beaches plus the Uluwatu sunset, without packing the night before your airport run.

Why it wins: Canggu is a scene, not a sight. Cafés, beach clubs, a black-sand beach and a great many scooters. It's genuinely enjoyable and it does not improve with a second day, especially in a month when its surf is the worst version of itself.

Why an hour on a Bali map is two hours on a Bali road

Ubud to Canggu is about 35 km and takes 75–90 minutes. Canggu to Uluwatu is about 40 km and takes 90 minutes, sometimes two hours, because it routes around the airport through Bali's worst traffic. Nothing on this island is far and everything takes a long time — single-lane roads, no bypass worth the name, and traffic that local operators describe as at record levels.

This is why a third base costs more than one extra hotel check-in. Each move burns a mid-day, which in November is the only reliably dry part of the day you have.

What both shapes skip, and why

Nusa Penida. The Kelingking cliff photograph is the most-posted image in Indonesia. Getting it is a 45-minute fast-boat crossing each way, then three or four hours in a pickup on genuinely broken roads, then a queue at the viewpoint. In the wet season the crossing gets rough and cancellations are common. It needs an overnight to be worth it and you don't have one.

North and east Bali. Sidemen, Amed, Tulamben, Lovina, Munduk — the half of the island that isn't a beach resort. Amed and Tulamben are especially tempting in November because the east coast stays calm while the west is churning, and the USAT Liberty wreck at Tulamben is a shore dive straight off the beach. But it's three hours each way from anywhere you're sleeping.

The rest of Indonesia. Java, Flores, Sulawesi, Raja Ampat. See §06.

06 Scale

Bali is not
Indonesia

Twenty-seven countries in nineteen weeks means every stop is a sample rather than a survey, and mostly that's fine. Indonesia is the stop where the gap between the sample and the country is largest: one island out of roughly 17,000, four million-odd people out of 280 million, and the only Hindu-majority province in the world's largest Muslim-majority nation. Bali is not representative of Indonesia in almost any respect, which is worth knowing so you don't come away thinking you've seen it.

What five nights in Bali is standing in for

Java — Borobudur and Prambanan, Yogyakarta, the Ijen sulphur miners, Bromo at dawn. A week on its own, and the obvious first add if this trip ever gets a sequel.

Flores and Komodo — the eastern islands, and the sharpest reminder of the scale: the M7.7 earthquake that struck off Ende, Flores on 14 August 2026, killing more than fifty people and displacing thousands, happened about 600 km east of Bali. Same country, entirely different world, and Bali was unaffected.

Sulawesi and Raja Ampat — Tana Toraja's funeral architecture; the best reef diving on the planet. Both are multi-flight commitments from Bali.

None of this is an argument against the stop. Four days is enough for one island done properly and nowhere near enough for two done badly. It's an argument for calling it what it is.

Seismic and volcanic, plainly

Indonesia sits on the Ring of Fire and this is not an abstraction: 2026 has already brought the Flores quake above, ongoing aftershocks, and eruption activity at Anak Krakatau and Semeru that disrupted flights. Bali's own risk on your dates is low, and none of this is a reason to change the plan.

The one practical consequence is your outbound. Ngurah Rai has closed for volcanic ash before — a Java or Lombok eruption drifting the wrong way is what does it, and it can shut the airport for a day with very little notice. Given you're connecting straight into Manila on a pre-dawn flight: don't book anything non-refundable in Manila for the 25th, and check that your travel insurance covers ash-cloud disruption specifically, since some policies exclude it.

07 Getting around

Driver, app,
or scooter

There is no train, no metro and no useful bus. Everything is a car, a scooter, or a very slow walk. The real decision on each day is whether to hire a driver for the whole day or take rides one at a time — and unlike most places, the app isn't automatically cheaper, because Grab and Gojek are blocked in exactly the places you want to be. Where the apps don't work

Grab and Gojek are legal and widely used, but local driver cooperatives enforce their own turf and the platforms quietly respect it. Central Ubud — the Monkey Forest and palace area — is local-driver-only in practice. Canggu's beach clubs post security who turn app cars away at the gate. The Uluwatu temples and the remote Bukit beaches are served almost entirely by local co-ops at fixed, higher rates. Add surge on top: rain, sunset and Canggu at rush hour can triple a fare, and all three happen at once most afternoons in November.

The practical upshot is that a driver for the day is more often the right answer here than it would be almost anywhere else on this trip — not because it's cheap, but because it's the version that works everywhere and waits for you in the rain.

Scooters and the licence problemRental is Rp 80,000–150,000 a day and everyone does it. Legally you need an International Driving Permit with a motorcycle endorsement plus the equivalent category on your Canadian licence, and Bali police have been running checkpoints on exactly this since 2025. Without it you're unlicensed: a fine at best, and — the part that matters — your travel insurance almost certainly voids the medical claim. Bali's road-injury statistics for tourists are grim. In the wet season, on unfamiliar roads, for four days, it's a bad trade.

Airport transfersPre-booked fixed rates beat the metered taxi rank: roughly Rp 475,000 to Ubud against Rp 600,000-plus metered in traffic, and about Rp 250,000 to Seminyak against Rp 300,000-plus. Book the Ubud leg ahead so someone is holding a sign when you land, and book the 03:00 airport run the day before — apps are thin at that hour.

Traffic timings, honestlyAirport→Ubud 90–120 min. Ubud→Canggu 75–90. Canggu→Uluwatu 90–120. Uluwatu→airport 60 in daylight, 45 at three in the morning. Add 30% to anything crossing the south between 16:00 and 19:00.

WalkingUbud's centre is walkable and pleasant. Canggu is walkable and unpleasant — no pavements, constant scooters. The Bukit is not walkable at all; the beaches are separate cliff staircases connected by road.

Temple etiquetteSarong and sash at every temple — usually provided or rented at the gate for a small fee, and Uluwatu includes it. Cover shoulders. Don't stand in front of people praying or climb on shrines for a photograph. Menstruating women are traditionally asked not to enter temple grounds; signs say so at the gate.

Offerings underfootThe little palm-leaf trays of flowers and rice on every doorstep and pavement are canang sari, placed fresh three times a day. Step around them, never over or on them. Once you notice them you'll see thousands.

EatingThe best food is Rp 30,000–60,000 at a warung: nasi campur, babi guling in Ubud, grilled fish on the Bukit. Jimbaran's beach seafood is priced by the kilo — agree the weight and the price before it goes on the grill, every time.

What to pack for itA light rain shell you'll actually wear, sandals that survive being soaked, one long-sleeved layer for temples, and shoes with grip — the cliff staircases at Bingin and Nyang Nyang are steep and they're wet in November.
