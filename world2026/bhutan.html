01 The entry fee

The only country
with a turnstile

Every other stop on this trip charges you nothing to exist inside it. Bhutan charges US$100 per person per night — the Sustainable Development Fee — plus a US$40 visa fee, before you have booked a bed or eaten a meal. That is deliberate: the policy is "high value, low volume", and the fee is the volume control. On four nights it comes to US$440 a head just to be admitted.

The good news is timing. The SDF was US$200 a night until September 2023; the 50% discount that brought it to $100 runs through 31 August 2027, so your window sits comfortably inside the cheap era. The other thing worth knowing before you build a budget: a licensed Bhutanese guide is not optional for most of what you came to see, so the guide and vehicle are effectively a second entry fee. What the SDF is and isn't

It is a government levy, paid up front with the visa application, that funds free healthcare and schooling, forest cover above 70%, and trail and monument upkeep. It is not a tour package: it buys you no hotel, no meals, no guide and no transport. Those are all separate, and Bhutan has no hostel-and-local-bus underlayer to fall back on the way Thailand does.

Children under 6 travel free; ages 6–11 pay half. Indian nationals pay Nu 1,200 a night on a separate permit system. Day-trippers who stay inside the border towns of Phuentsholing, Samtse, Gelephu or Samdrup Jongkhar and leave within 24 hours are exempt — irrelevant to you, since you arrive by air into the interior.

The counting rule is per night, not per day. Arriving 7 November and flying out on the 11th is four nights, so four SDF nights: US$400. Don't let an operator quote you five.

The one genuine way to spend less

Since September 2023 you are no longer required to book through a licensed tour operator — you can apply for the visa yourself on the government portal and hire a guide directly, which typically runs US$40–50 a day against the US$150–200 a day an all-in package charges. That difference over four days is real money.

The catch is that you then arrange your own hotel, vehicle, driver and permits, in a country where almost nothing is bookable on the platforms you're used to and where a wrong guess about which monument needs a guide costs you the monument. On a five-day stop wedged between Bangkok and Kathmandu, an operator handling the logistics is defensible. On a five-week stay it obviously isn't. Decide which you are.

02 Getting in

A 05:00 flight
into a valley

Bangkok sits immediately before Bhutan on this itinerary for exactly one reason: Drukair flies BKK–PBH direct, and there are very few doors into this country. That convenience comes with an alarm clock. Drukair's KB153 leaves Bangkok at 05:00 and lands in Paro at 07:15 on the July–November 2026 timetable — which means leaving your Bangkok hotel around 02:30, on the last night of stop 05.

7 November is a Saturday

Check this first, before anything else

Drukair publishes its schedule in monthly PDFs, usually three to six months out, and the operating days move. The March 2026 timetable ran KB153 six days a week — Monday through Friday plus Sunday, with Saturday the one day it did not fly. Agents currently advertise the route as daily. Both cannot be true for every month.

Your arrival day is a Saturday. If November 2026 repeats the March pattern, the direct flight you have planned around does not exist on the date you need it.

What to doPull the November 2026 monthly schedule PDF from Drukair's own site and confirm KB153 on Sat 7 Nov before you commit to Bangkok's dates. If Saturday is blank, the fixes in order of preference: shift the whole leg forward to Sun 8 Nov and take a night off Nepal; fly Bhutan Airlines, which added BKK–PBH in 2026 (B3701, 06:30→09:55, and a far more civilised departure); or route BKK–Kolkata/Bagdogra/Guwahati and pick up a Drukair regional leg, which costs you the day.

Paro is a visual approach

Plan for a delay, not a disaster

The airport sits at 2,235 m in a river valley ringed by peaks to 5,500 m, on a single 1,964 m runway. There is no instrument landing system and no radar. Every arrival and departure is flown by eye, in daylight only, with the crew sighting the runway a mile or two out and seconds to commit. Roughly 50 pilots worldwide are certified for it.

What that means practically: cloud, valley fog or high wind cancels flights, and there is no night operation to recover into. Delays cluster in the monsoon, June to September — you are arriving in the clearest, calmest stretch of the year, which is the single best argument for this window.

The leverSit on the left flying in — the Himalaya are north of the track from Bangkok, Kathmandu and Delhi alike — and on the right flying out. Online check-in opens two days ahead; set an alarm for it, because there are 118 seats and the window rows go.

Leaving for Kathmandu

Drukair's KB400 Paro→Kathmandu runs daily, departing between roughly 07:00 and 08:00 depending on the day and landing an hour later. It is a short hop and the most spectacular scheduled flight in the world: Kanchenjunga, Makalu, Lhotse and Everest strung along the northern horizon. Sit on the right. Clear November air is the whole reason this leg is worth a window seat and a charged phone.

One consequence for your last day: an 07:00-ish departure means being at the airport around 06:00 and awake at 05:00, in the dark, at freezing point. Wednesday 11 November is not a day in Bhutan. Treat Tuesday evening as the end of the stop and pack the night before.

03 The time audit

Five days
is four days

Bhutan is the rare stop where the arrival day survives. You land at 07:15, clear a small airport, and are in Paro town before nine — that is a real day, not a fragment. The departure day is the one that vanishes, because the only flight to Kathmandu goes at dawn. So: four usable days, four nights, one of them at altitude before you climb anything.

The daylight budgetParo in mid-November: sunrise 06:22, sunset 17:11. Under eleven hours of light, and the valleys go dark early because the walls are high. This is the real constraint on the stop — not the number of days but the number of usable hours inside them. Nothing gets scheduled after four in the afternoon except dinner.

No jet lag to exploitBangkok is UTC+7, Bhutan UTC+6. You lose an hour and gain nothing. Unlike Japan, dawn here is not free — you have to actually get up. Given the daylight budget, do it anyway.

Acclimatisation is the reason for day oneYou go from sea level in Bangkok to 2,235 m in three hours of flying. That is not altitude-sickness territory for most people, but Tiger's Nest tops out at 3,120 m and 520 m of climbing feels different on your first afternoon than on your second morning. Day one stays in the valley on purpose.

04 The open question

Punakha:
in or out

Paro, Thimphu and Tiger's Nest are the fixed points, and they fit four days with room to breathe. Punakha — the old winter capital, the country's most beautiful dzong, at a warm 1,200 m — is the obvious addition and the obvious temptation. It costs two mountain drives over the Dochu La pass at 3,100 m and, realistically, all of Thimphu.

Recommended

Paro & Thimphu

Paro ×2→ Thimphu ×1→ Paro ×1→ PBH

Two nights in Paro puts Tiger's Nest on your second morning — acclimatised, and with two spare days behind it if the weather closes in. Then a night in Thimphu, a proper one, with an evening in the only capital city on earth without a traffic light. Back to Paro on Tuesday for the dawn flight.

What the slack buys you: Chele La. At 3,988 m it's the highest motorable pass in Bhutan, two hours from Paro through blue-pine forest, with Jomolhari on the skyline on a clear morning and the Haa valley dropping away behind it. In November it is exactly the day this window exists for.

Monday becomes a drive: Paro to Thimphu, up to Dochu La and its 108 chortens, then down the far side to Punakha — roughly four hours of mountain road each way with stops. Punakha Dzong at the confluence of the Pho and Mo rivers genuinely is the finest building in the country, and the descent to 1,200 m is a warm, subtropical relief after two nights at altitude.

The cost is Thimphu's, entirely. You see it through a windscreen twice and get maybe two hours there on the way back. Chele La is gone. And you spend two of four usable days largely in a car on a road that narrows and switchbacks past the pass.

Why the recommendation keeps Thimphu rather than Punakha

Because of what the rest of this trip already contains. You will see spectacular religious architecture in Cambodia, Nepal, Sri Lanka and Egypt; Punakha Dzong is superb but it is competing against Angkor six weeks later. What you will not see anywhere else on twenty-seven stops is a functioning Himalayan capital of 120,000 people — archery in a public field, monks with smartphones, the traffic officer in his pavilion, a city that decided to stay small on purpose. That is the thing that is only here.

The other half of the argument is weather insurance. Tiger's Nest is the reason this stop exists, it needs a clear-ish morning, and a plan with two spare days can absorb one bad one. The Punakha shape leaves you a single fallback day, and if you lose it you have paid US$440 to look at cloud.

What's out of reach either way, and worth knowing why

Phobjikha and the black-necked cranes. The cranes arrive from Tibet in late October and winter in the Phobjikha valley, and the Black-Necked Crane Festival falls on 11 November 2026 at Gangtey Goemba — your departure day, five hours' drive from Paro. This is a genuine near-miss rather than an oversight. Reaching it would mean adding two nights and abandoning the Kathmandu leg; the cranes are magnificent and the arithmetic doesn't work.

Bumthang and Jambay Lhakhang Drup. The fire ceremony, embers walked barefoot, late October into early November. It is eight hours east of Thimphu by road or a domestic flight to Bathpalathang. Not in five days.

Trekking. The Druk Path from Paro to Thimphu is four to six days over 4,200 m passes and is the correct way to see this country. You do not have four to six days, and this trip has Poon Hill in Nepal in exactly one week's time. Bank it.

07 The climb

Tiger's Nest,
properly

Taktsang Palphug clings to a cliff face 900 m above the Paro valley floor, where Guru Rinpoche is said to have arrived on the back of a flying tigress in the eighth century. It is the reason most people come to Bhutan and it is not a viewpoint you drive to — it is 6 to 6.4 km round trip, four to six hours, and a net 520 m of climbing that includes a hard sting at the end.

Indicative profile · the numbered sting is the staircase down to the bridge and straight back up — you climb it twice, once in each direction

The timing that matters

Start 07:30–08:30

The monastery admits visitors 08:00–13:00 and again 14:00–17:00, closed over the middle of the day. Start at half seven and you reach the entrance inside the morning window with the light still low and the coach groups behind you.

Start at ten and you arrive during the closure, having climbed 520 m to sit outside a locked door for an hour. This is the single most common way the day gets ruined.

AlsoEntry is Nu 1,000 for international visitors, Nu 500 for ages 5–18. Your guide must be with you. Cameras, phones and bags go into lockers at the entrance — photography inside is genuinely prohibited, and the inside is the part worth remembering rather than filming.

Horses, and their limits

Uphill only, to halfway

Ponies can be hired at the trailhead for roughly US$18–25 one way, and they take you to the cafeteria at 2,940 m and no further. The upper section — the viewpoint, the staircase down to the waterfall bridge, the flights back up to the entrance — is on foot for everyone.

They are also not permitted for the descent, which is the half that actually hurts your knees. If you take a horse up you still walk the whole way down.

The cafeteriaMidway at 2,940 m, with meals around Nu 600–900, water and the only toilets on the route. Most people eat on the way back rather than the way up. Take two litres of water regardless; it's dry, it's high, and you will drink more than you expect at 10°C.

Why it goes on your second morning

One night at 2,235 m before climbing to 3,120 m is not real acclimatisation, but it is meaningfully better than stepping off a Bangkok flight and starting up the hill the same afternoon — and the afternoon is the wrong half of the day anyway. Sunday 8 November gives you a full morning window with two clear days behind it as insurance.

The other reason is weather. November mornings in Paro are the most reliable of the year, but the valley can hold cloud, and the photograph you want — the monastery floating with nothing behind it — needs the cliff face clear. If Sunday looks wrong, move it to Monday or Tuesday. A plan that can do that is worth more here than a plan that squeezes in one more dzong.

08 The window

Why early
November

This is the clearest, driest, stillest stretch of the Bhutanese year, and unlike Bali later on this trip there is no compromise to admit. The monsoon has gone, the winter cloud has not arrived, and the air over the Himalaya is as transparent as it gets. The cost is temperature: Paro runs about 10°C by day and −1°C at night, and the valley is in shadow by half five.

Rain is essentially off the tableAbout a 21% chance of any precipitation on a given November day in Paro, and roughly 13 mm for the whole month against July's downpours. November is also the calmest month of the year for wind, which is why flights into Paro behave themselves.

Cold at both ends of the dayFreezing or just below overnight, and Bhutanese hotels below the top tier are often heated by a single bukhari wood stove in the common room. Pack for a mountain, not for the Asia you have been in since October: thermal base layer, insulated mid-layer, hat and gloves for the pre-dawn airport run.

The clarity is the pointThe whole reason Bhutan sits between Bangkok and Kathmandu on this route is that early November buys you sharp air over big mountains — Jomolhari from Chele La, the range from the Kathmandu flight, and the cliff face at Taktsang standing out instead of dissolving into haze. That is a fortnight-wide window and you are inside it.

The festival you just miss, and the holiday you land on

Bhutan's tsechu calendar runs on the lunar year, and 7–11 November 2026 falls between the big ones. Jambay Lhakhang Drup in Bumthang — the fire ceremony, embers underfoot — lands late October into early November, but it is eight hours east. The Black-Necked Crane Festival at Gangtey is 11 November, the morning your flight leaves for Kathmandu, five hours' drive the wrong way. Both are real misses; neither is fixable inside this itinerary.

What you do land on: Wednesday 11 November is a national holiday — the Birth Anniversary of the Fourth Druk Gyalpo, marked as Constitution Day. Government offices close. It affects nothing on your last morning except that if you had any admin to do in Thimphu, Tuesday is the deadline. Note also that 1 November, the King's Coronation Day and the Descending Day of Lord Buddha, falls just before you arrive.

EatingEma datshi — chillies and yak cheese — is the national dish and it is genuinely hot; ask for it mild the first time and build up. Red rice, momos, phaksha paa, suja butter tea, and ara if someone offers it. Restaurant culture is thin outside Thimphu, so most dinners will be at your hotel, and that is normal rather than a failure of planning.

Booking windowNovember is Bhutan's peak season alongside April, and the country has a hard cap on decent rooms. Flights, visa, guide and hotels all want to be locked six to eight weeks out at minimum. Nothing here is a last-minute country.

The corridor rule, preciselyFree movement inside Paro, Thimphu and Punakha means walking around, eating, shopping, a stroll along the Pa Chhu. It does not mean entering a dzong alone, and it does not extend to Chele La, Haa or anywhere over a pass. Plan your guide's days accordingly rather than assuming you can improvise them.

What you can't do hereNo independent driving — hire cars come with a driver. No unaccompanied trekking. No drones without a permit that you will not get in time. And no rushing: distances look tiny and the roads are single-lane switchbacks, so double whatever a map tells you.
