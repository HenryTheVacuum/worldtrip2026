01 The time audit

Five days
is three days

Bhutan is the one country on this trip where the calendar and the invoice disagree in your favour and the calendar and the itinerary disagree against you. You are charged for four nights — arrival night counts, departure night doesn't — but the Drukair schedule takes both ends of the window. Bangkok departs before dawn and Paro to Kathmandu goes at first light on the 11th, so what you actually get is three full days and one jet-lagged afternoon.

The altitude tax on day oneYou leave Bangkok at 2 metres and are standing in Paro at 2,235 metres two and three-quarter hours later. That is a faster ascent than any trekker would choose. Day one is not a wasted day, but it should be a flat one — a valley temple, a dzong, an early dinner. The mountain is on day two for a reason.

Why the SDF clock is kinder than the calendarThe Sustainable Development Fee is charged per night stayed, and the night you fly out is not one of them. Four nights — 7, 8, 9 and 10 November — at US$100 each. Unused nights are refundable by the Department of Immigration after departure, minus the bank charges.

Arriving from Bangkok is deliberateDrukair's BKK–Paro service is the reason Thailand sits immediately before this stop. It is also the reason this leg has almost no slack: the route runs three to four times a week, not daily, and Paro is closed to instrument approaches. Both facts are dealt with in section 07.

02 Two dates that decide the week

Tuesday, and
the 11th

Almost everything about a Bhutan itinerary is negotiable with your operator. These two are not, and both land inside your five days. Read them before you agree a single itinerary draft.

Tiger's Nest is closed on Tuesdays

10 November is a Tuesday

Paro Taktsang closes to visitors one day a week. Your window is Sat 7 to Wed 11 November — which leaves Sunday the 8th and Monday the 9th as the only two days the monastery is open to you, and Wednesday is a dawn flight out.

This is the single reason you are in Bhutan. It is also weather-dependent, altitude-dependent and roughly five hours of uphill walking at 3,000 metres on your second day at altitude. Planning it with no reserve day is planning to maybe not do it.

The fixHike it Sunday 8 November and keep Monday the 9th free as the backup. Every routing option in section 03 is built around protecting that reserve — which is exactly what the Punakha detour spends.

The Black-Necked Crane Festival

One day out of reach

Held every year on 11 November at Gangtey Goenpa in the Phobjikha valley: masked cham dances by the monks, then schoolchildren dancing the cranes back for the winter. It is the warmest, least touristic festival in the Bhutanese calendar. It is also on your departure date, when you will be airborne to Kathmandu by about eight in the morning.

Even without the flight, Phobjikha is five to six hours' drive each way from Paro over Dochula — a two-night commitment out of four.

Worth knowing anyway11 November is also a national holiday, the birth anniversary of the Fourth Druk Gyalpo and Constitution Day. Dzongs and government offices close. You're leaving at dawn, so it costs you nothing — but don't plan a last morning around anything official.

03 The open question

Punakha:
in or out

Paro is fixed — it's where you land, where you leave from, and where Taktsang is. Thimphu is an hour away and effectively free. The real decision is whether you push over the Dochula pass to Punakha, which is widely held to hold the most beautiful building in the country and costs roughly seven hours in a car to reach and return.

Recommended

Paro & Thimphu

Paro ×2→ Thimphu ×1→ Paro ×1→ PBH

Two nights in Paro up front, so you sleep at 2,235 m before you climb to 3,120 m, and so Monday the 9th stays free as the Taktsang reserve day. Thimphu on the 9th or 10th for the Buddha Dordenma, the market and Tashichho Dzong lit up after the offices close, then back to Paro for the pre-dawn flight.

The consolation: Dochula pass itself — 108 chortens on a 3,100 m ridge with the eastern Himalaya behind them on a clear November morning — is a two-hour round trip from Thimphu. You get the view without the valley.

Push to Punakha

Why the recommendation protects Monday rather than spending it

On a normal trip a lost day is an annoyance. Here it is terminal: the Tuesday closure means a missed Sunday cannot be made up on Tuesday, and Wednesday you are gone before sunrise. Add the fact that you arrive at 2,235 m directly from sea level and are asked to climb 900 vertical metres roughly 24 hours later, and the probability that day two goes wrong is not small.

Punakha is genuinely the more beautiful building. But Punakha will still be there, and a four-night visit to Bhutan that gets Taktsang is a success while one that doesn't is a very expensive near-miss. If you are confident about altitude and the forecast is settled by the time you land, the Punakha shape is a real option — decide it on the ground on the 8th, not in Toronto in advance.

What's cut either way

Phobjikha and the cranes. Five to six hours each way, and the festival falls on your departure date regardless. Section 02 has the detail.

Bumthang and central Bhutan. The spiritual heartland, four monastic valleys, and either a long domestic flight or two days of mountain road. Not in four nights.

Any trek. Even the short Bumdra or Druk Path routes want three to five days and a separate permit. The Taktsang day walk is your trek.

Chele La pass. At 3,988 m it's the highest motorable road in the country and the drive out to Haa is superb — but it's a full day from Paro and it competes directly with the Taktsang reserve day.

06 The altitude question

Sea level to
3,120 in a day

This is the highest you go on the entire westbound trip until Poon Hill next week, and you reach it from Bangkok — two metres above the sea — in under three hours of flying. Nobody gets seriously ill at these numbers, but plenty of people have a bad headache and a slow Sunday, which on a four-night visit is expensive.

What the numbers meanParo town is 2,235 m and Thimphu 2,334 m — high enough to be short of breath on stairs, low enough that almost nobody gets properly ill. The Taktsang trail starts around 2,600 m, passes the cafeteria at roughly 2,940 m and tops out at the monastery at 3,120 m. Dochula pass, if you go, is about 3,100 m but you're sitting in a car.

The two things that workSleep at Paro's altitude for a night before you climb — which is the whole argument for the recommended shape — and drink far more water than feels necessary. Bhutan's air in November is cold and bone dry, and dehydration counterfeits every symptom of altitude.

The hike itselfEight kilometres round trip, five to six hundred metres of gain, four to six hours including time inside. It isn't technical; it's a relentless staircase. Ponies can carry you as far as the cafeteria and no further — the final stretch, down into the gorge and back up the cliff stairs, is on foot for everyone.

Then NepalYou leave for Kathmandu at 1,400 m and are on the Poon Hill trail within days at over 3,200 m. The five days in Bhutan are, incidentally, the best possible preparation for stop 07 — arrive part-acclimatised rather than starting from scratch.

07 The window

The clearest
month there is

Bhutan gets an Indian-subcontinent monsoon: June to September the western valleys take three-quarters of their annual rain, cloud sits on the ridges, and Paro's visual-only approach starts diverting aircraft to Kolkata. Then it stops. November is the driest, clearest month of the year — roughly 15 mm of rain across the whole month against 350 in July.

What to expectParo and Thimphu run roughly 12–17°C by day and close to freezing at night in early November, with hard blue skies and very low humidity. The sun at 2,300 m is fierce; the moment it drops behind a ridge it is genuinely cold. Layers, a proper warm midlayer, and sun cream you will not expect to need.

Peak season, honestlyOctober and November are Bhutan's busiest months, and this is the one place on the trip where that barely matters — the whole tourism model is built to cap numbers. Taktsang's trail is the exception and it thins out dramatically if you're on it by seven.

The flight-delay riskLow in November but not zero. Paro is a visual approach into a valley ringed by 5,500 m peaks, daylight-hours only, flown by a small certified pilot pool. Morning fog on departure and afternoon cloud on arrival are the usual causes. It matters most on the 11th: a Kathmandu misconnection cascades into stop 07.

The dry-air problemEveryone underestimates it. Cold, thin, dry mountain air will crack your lips, wreck your sinuses and dehydrate you faster than Bangkok's heat did. Lip balm and a litre more water a day than feels sensible.

08 The cost floor

What four nights
actually costs

Bhutan is the only country on this trip with a legislated minimum spend. The Sustainable Development Fee is US$100 per person per night — halved from US$200 in September 2023, and confirmed at the discounted rate through 31 August 2027, so your window is safely inside it. What the SDF is and isn't

It is a government fee that funds healthcare, education and forest cover — not a tour price. It buys you nothing: no hotel, no guide, no meals, no entrance tickets. Those are all separate and all still payable. Tourists routinely see "US$100 a day" and budget accordingly, then discover the real all-in number is three times that.

It is paid up front, through the official portal, at visa-application time — your visa cannot be processed until the SDF has cleared. Nights you don't end up using are refundable by the Department of Immigration after you leave, less bank charges. Nights you add — a weather delay, say — are chargeable at the same rate.

Children pay half from 6 to 11 and nothing under 6. Indian, Bangladeshi and Maldivian nationals pay different rates entirely. None of that applies to you.

The guide question, plainly

Sources disagree and it matters, so here is the honest version. Bhutan's official tourism FAQ states that a local guide is mandatory at all times while travelling in Bhutan, including for visitors driving their own vehicle, and that a guide is required to enter monuments and dzongs. Many operators describe a softer reality in which you can wander Paro and Thimphu town, eat, and shop unaccompanied, with the guide required for every actual sight.

Either way, the practical answer for a four-night visit is the same: you will have a licensed guide and a driver, arranged through a licensed operator, for essentially all of it. Tiger's Nest specifically cannot be visited without one. Budget US$40–50 a day for the guide and US$40–60 for a vehicle with driver, or take a bundled package at roughly US$250 a day which folds in hotels, meals, entries and both.

09 Getting in and out

The thin
air bridge

Paro has one runway, 1,900 metres of it, at 2,235 m in a valley walled by peaks over 5,000 m. There is no instrument approach — it is flown visually, in daylight only, by a small pool of specifically certified pilots. Two airlines serve it and nobody else may. This is the constraint that shapes both ends of your stay.

Bangkok–Paro isn't daily

Verify Saturday 7 November

Drukair runs BKK–PBH roughly three to four times a week, not every day, and published schedules for the route cluster on Monday, Wednesday, Thursday and Sunday. 7 November 2026 is a Saturday. Bhutan Airlines has been adding capacity on the route from September 2026, which may change the picture — but it is not something to assume.

Drukair publishes its schedule three to six months out, as monthly PDFs. For a November departure that means the timetable should be firm well before you need to commit.

Do this firstBefore anything else on this leg, confirm a Saturday 7 November BKK→PBH departure exists. If it doesn't, the cheapest fix is shifting Bangkok by a day — Thailand is three nights of city with no fixed bookings, and Bhutan is four nights of paid-in-advance SDF. Move the flexible one.

Paro–Kathmandu

Daily, and the best hour of the trip

Both Drukair and Bhutan Airlines fly PBH–KTM daily, around 55 to 90 minutes, departing early — roughly 06:50 to 08:30. That's what consumes 11 November, and why the Crane Festival is out of reach.

It is also, on a clear November morning, the finest scheduled flight in Asia: the aircraft runs west along the spine of the Himalaya with Kangchenjunga, Makalu, Lhotse and Everest lined up out of the windows.

The leverSit on the right-hand side flying west to Kathmandu — the range is to the north. Coming in from Bangkok it's the reverse: left-side window seats face the mountains. Check in the moment check-in opens; the good seats go instantly.

Practical flight notes

Baggage. Both carriers give 30 kg checked in economy plus 5–7 kg cabin — generous by regional standards and useful on a trip this long.

Fares. Bangkok–Paro return typically runs US$400–700 depending on season and how early you book; one-way pricing on these routes is proportionally worse than most airlines. Kathmandu is short and cheaper. Book three to four months ahead for November, which is peak.

Booking order. Flights first, then the operator and SDF, then the visa — the visa needs the SDF cleared and the itinerary fixed, and the review takes five working days once submitted. Nothing here is a same-week job.

Delays. If Paro closes on the 11th, your Kathmandu arrival slips and so does everything downstream — plus another US$100 of SDF for the unplanned night. Worth checking that your Nepal accommodation on the 11th is free to cancel.

EatingEma datshi — chillies stewed in cheese — is the national dish and is genuinely, seriously hot; it is not a garnish, it's the main course. Red rice, buckwheat noodles and dumplings round it out. Hotel restaurants tend to serve a cautious buffet aimed at tourists; ask your guide to take you somewhere he'd actually eat.

EtiquetteWalk clockwise around chortens, prayer wheels and mani walls. Shoulders and knees covered in every religious building, hats and sunglasses off. Don't photograph inside monasteries. Point with an open hand, never a finger.

The hot stone bathRiver stones heated in a fire and dropped into a wooden tub of water and artemisia. Arrange one for the evening after Taktsang — it is the correct use of Sunday night and your legs will need it.

What this leg feedsFour nights at 2,200–3,100 m is the single best preparation you could have for Nepal. You land in Kathmandu on the 11th already part-acclimatised, with Poon Hill five days later at 3,210 m. That adjacency is the whole reason Bhutan sits here in the running order.
